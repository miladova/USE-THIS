*This is a work of fiction. Arwen Prys Vaughan, her family, and the passengers and crew who surround her at close range are invented. Harold Godfrey Lowe was a real man and the ship was a real ship; where the record is firm I have tried to be faithful to it, and where the record is silent I have imagined. The officers of the* Titanic *— Smith, Wilde, Murdoch, Lightoller, Pitman, Boxhall, Lowe, Moody — were eight men on a ship built to be unsinkable. Four of them lived.*

# I

## A Ship Going Somewhere

### Southampton and Cherbourg · 10–11 April 1912

The porter said her name wrong, and Arwen did not correct him.

"Mrs. Vorn? First class, is it? This way, ma'am, if you please."

He was a small man with a moustache like a badly tied knot and he had already taken her dressing case out of her hand without asking, so there was nothing to do but follow him. Behind her, Nesta came sideways up the gangway, hauling the wicker basket she had refused to give up to anybody, her hat knocked crooked by the wind, nineteen years old and furious about it in a way that was entirely silent.

The gangway flexed underfoot. That was the first thing Arwen noticed — that a thing so vast could flex. Somewhere below, in the grey water of Southampton Docks, a tug was complaining. The air was a compound of coal smoke, river mud, new paint, and something sweeter underneath it, a smell like a piano shop, which she later learned was varnish, four hundred gallons of it, still curing.

"Boat deck first, ma'am, then down the grand staircase—"

"I know the way."

"Of course, ma'am."

She did not, in fact, know the way, but she knew the shape of the way, which was enough. She had crossed the Atlantic eleven times: on the *Umbria*, on the *Etruria*, on the *Lucania* with Emlyn in '05 when the second-class saloon had smelled of boiled cabbage for four days and Emlyn had made a joke of it every single one of them. She had crossed on the *Oceanic*, on the *Majestic*, on the *Teutonic*. She was a woman who had been seasick in the Bay of Biscay in three separate decades and had opinions about which side of a ship was best in a following sea.

But she had never boarded a ship that smelled like a piano shop.

*Titanic.* She looked up at the funnels, and then wished she hadn't, because looking up at them made her feel like a child in a cathedral, and she was twenty-nine years old and a widow and had buried a husband and a mother and a stillborn daughter, and she did not care to be made to feel like a child in a cathedral by a piece of sheet steel.

"Madam," said the porter, "mind the—"

She minded the step. She went through the door, and the wind stopped, and the world became a corridor.

It was not like a corridor. It was like a corridor in a hotel that had been built by people who had never seen a hotel but had been told about one, and had decided to improve on the description. There was carpet the colour of claret. There was a handrail of polished mahogany with a brass fitting every eighteen inches, and each brass fitting was polished enough to hold a small brown reflection of her face. There were electric lamps in the shape of lilies. There was a smell of beeswax and, faintly, of oranges.

"Mrs. Vaughan." A steward, older than the porter, with a face like a well-used map. "Your cabins are ready. B-57 and B-59. Your steward is Mr. Penney. He'll bring your luggage directly."

"Thank you."

"Very good, madam. And — the sailing's at noon. There's a deal of coal being taken on yet. It may be nearer half past."

"Thank you," she said again, and thought: *coal.* Of course. There had been a coal strike in the spring and every ship in the North Atlantic had been chasing fuel like a spinster chasing a curate, and the White Star Line had emptied half the bunkers in Southampton into this one to get her away on time. That was the sort of thing Emlyn would have known without being told. Emlyn would have looked at the smoke and said a number, and the number would have been right.

She did not want to think about Emlyn. It was inconvenient, in the fourth year of her widowhood, to think about him at all, and yet the ship insisted. The ship was the sort of thing he would have loved. He would have walked her decks with his hat tipped back and his hands behind him and said, in that voice that made everything sound like a bet he had already won, *Well now, Arwen fach. What d'you make of this?*

*I make of it,* she thought, following the steward down a stairway wide enough to drive a pony up, *that I have spent nine hundred pounds on a cabin I will occupy for six days, because a man in New York asked me to marry him and I said yes and then did not go.*

That was not quite fair to Cedric. She had gone. She was going now. She had gone to Wales for her mother's anniversary, and then to London to see about the lease on the Bedford Square house, and then — because it was April, and because the spring in London was making her feel like a room that had been shut up — she had written to Cedric and told him she would take the new ship rather than the *Mauretania*, and he had written back that this was an excellent idea and that he would meet her at the Cunard pier on the eighteenth and that she was not to lift a finger about the wedding, he had it all in hand, there was nothing to it.

*Nothing to it.* Six weeks to a wedding and there was nothing to it.

Her cabin was not a cabin. It was a room, with a fireplace that did not work, and a bed the size of a pony trap, and a wardrobe tall enough to hang a coat and a doubt in at the same time. The bedspread was a pale blue silk. The walls were panelled in a wood she did not recognise until she touched it and found it was painted to *look* like a wood she did not recognise.

"My God," said Nesta, from the doorway, and then, remembering herself: "Beg pardon, ma'am."

"Don't." Arwen sat down on the edge of the bed and bounced, once, experimentally. "It's like being inside a wedding cake."

"It's like being inside a *church*," said Nesta, and put the basket down. Inside it, wrapped in a flannel petticoat, was a teapot of her grandmother's that Nesta had decided, without consulting anyone, could not be left in Blaenau. "There's four kinds of soap in the bathroom. Four. I counted."

"Are they labelled?"

"In French."

"Then we're undone," said Arwen.

They unpacked for an hour. Nesta did the unpacking; Arwen did the sitting, and the looking out of the window at the coal barges, and the thinking about how strange it was that a person could be twenty-nine years old and have a whole life arranged for her, like furniture in a room she had not chosen, and be unable to say precisely which piece was the one she'd have thrown out.

At half past eleven she went up on deck.

---

The *Titanic* was moored at Berth 44 and the crowd below on the quay was not a crowd of passengers. It was a crowd of dock workers, carters, coal trimmers, and a thin bright line of the merely curious, standing behind a rope with their mouths open, and Arwen, leaning on the rail three decks above them, found that she could see into their mouths, and that the sight of so many open mouths made her feel obscurely ashamed.

Down at the waterline, a gang of men was casting off the last of the mooring lines. Somebody shouted. Somebody else shouted back, in the flat, cheerful, obscene way of dockers, and a hundred feet above them on the Boat Deck a band began to play.

She had not expected a band. Of all the things there were to expect — the coal, the smell, the flexing gangway — she had not expected a band, and the suddenness of it, a ragtime thing, brisk and slightly wrong, like a cheerful man at a funeral, made her eyes sting.

"You'll want to hold on to that rail in a minute," said a voice at her elbow.

She turned. It was a woman of about forty in a travelling coat of grey foulard, with a face that had been handsome and had decided to remain so, and a small dog under one arm, and a great deal of dark hair arranged with the air of an argument won.

"Will I?"

"They always come too close. Look — that one there, the *Oceanic*, and that little one in the middle, the *New York*. When we start to move the water goes out from under us like a tablecloth." The woman shifted the dog, which was a Brussels griffon and looked like a moustache with opinions. "In '98 I was on the *Normannia* out of Liverpool and we pulled a steamer clean round on her cables. Broke every window in her saloon. You could hear it from the rail."

"Then I shall hold on," said Arwen.

"Lavinia Thorne," said the woman. "Boston. That's my sister's dog; my sister has gone to be ill in her cabin and the dog is taking it personally."

"Arwen Vaughan. New York, by way of Wales."

"Ah," said Mrs. Thorne, in the tone of a woman fitting a key into a lock. "I thought there was something. You've got the vowels."

"I've got the vowels," Arwen agreed.

And then the *Titanic* began to move, and every single person on the deck said something at once.

It was not a shudder. That was the thing she would remember afterwards, on the deck, in the dark, with the ice at her feet: that the beginning had not been a shudder at all. It was a pressure. The whole ship pressed gently backwards, as if a hand had been laid flat on her bows, and then there was a sound from under the water — not a sound, a *sensation*, a long low grinding that came up through the deck plates and into the bones of Arwen's feet and stayed there, like a note held too long on an organ.

"Ha," said Mrs. Thorne, satisfied. "Here we go."

Down below, the little *New York* had begun to swing.

Arwen watched it happen the way one watches a glass slide off a table: with plenty of time and no ability whatever to stop it. The *New York*'s bow came round, slowly, majestically, and the gap between her and the *Titanic*'s flank closed from a street to a doorway, and men on the *New York*'s deck began to run in that useless way men run on ships. Somebody on the quay was screaming. Somebody else was laughing.

"Hold on," said Mrs. Thorne.

Arwen held on.

The tug *Vulcan* came thrashing out of nowhere with her funnel belching, and got a line on, and the *New York*'s bow stopped an arm's length from the *Titanic*'s paint — so close that a man could have reached out and touched her, and a man did reach out and touch her, a docker in his shirtsleeves, leaning out from the *New York*'s forecastle with one hand flat against the black plating of the largest moving object ever made by human beings, as casually as if he were steadying himself on a wall.

The crowd on the quay let out a noise like a sigh.

"Well," said Mrs. Thorne. "That's the excitement over. Have you lunched?"

"I haven't."

"Then come and have it, and tell me what brings a Welshwoman from New York onto a White Star boat in April, and I'll tell you what brings a Bostonwoman, and between us we'll have made up a better story than either."

They lunched. The dining saloon was a long room in the style of a Jacobean manor house, with a ceiling of plasterwork that had taken three men a year, and the menu was printed on card so thick that Arwen could have written a letter home on it. Mrs. Thorne ate like a woman who had made peace with her digestion, and talked like a woman who had made peace with everything else. Her husband had been in wool. Her husband had been dead six years. She had two sons, one of whom was doing something in Manila that she declined to specify, and she was going home to a house in Beacon Street that had eleven rooms and one occupant and was, she said, "like living in a hat."

"Now," she said, over the consommé, "you."

"There's nothing to me."

"There is always something to a woman who lunches alone in first class with a face like a shut book."

Arwen laughed. It surprised her. She had not laughed in — she could not immediately remember. "I'm to be married," she said.

"Ah." Mrs. Thorne's eyebrow did something complicated. "And?"

"And nothing. In June."

"And you don't care to be."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. You said 'I'm to be married' the way a woman says 'I'm to have a tooth out.'" She buttered a roll with great deliberation. "Is he a good man?"

"He is a very good man."

"Worse and worse."

"He's kind. He's patient. He has a house on Madison Avenue with a lift in it. He writes me every Tuesday without fail and he has never once in two years written anything in a letter that surprised me, and I have come to think that a life with a man who never surprises you might be — " She stopped.

"Might be what?"

"Restful," said Arwen.

Mrs. Thorne put down her knife. "My dear," she said, "I have been restful for six years and I can tell you that it is the single most exhausting thing a woman can do."

---

In the afternoon she asked a steward how many people were aboard, and he did not know, and she asked another one, and he did not know either, so she went to the purser's office and a clerk looked it up in a book and told her: two thousand two hundred and eight, counting the crew, of whom seven hundred and fifty were crew, and of the rest about three hundred and thirty in first class and two hundred and eighty in second and the remainder in steerage, and there were nine dogs and four hundred and forty tons of cargo and a motor car.

"Two thousand two hundred," said Arwen.

"Yes, madam."

"Thank you."

She walked out of the office with the number in her head and stood for a moment in the corridor and could not think what to do with it. It was too big to be a number of people. It was a number of sheep, or tons, or miles. She tried to picture them — a crowd at a football match, a crowd at a fair, the whole of Blaenau on a Saturday — and could not make it work, and in the end she did what she always did with a thing that was too big, which was to put it in a drawer in her mind and shut the drawer.

---

They raised Cherbourg at dusk, and it was the strangest hour of the whole day.

The *Titanic* did not come alongside. She was too big for the harbour, so she stood off, and out of the grey evening came two little tenders, the *Nomadic* and the *Traffic*, rolling like corks, and up the gangway came two hundred and seventy-four passengers and their trunks and their dogs and their servants, all of them soaked andwindblown and excited, and behind them the lights of Cherbourg came on one by one like someone walking through a house turning on lamps.

Arwen stood at the rail and watched the tenders come and go, and thought about how many last things a life could hold if you arranged them properly.

Nesta came up with a shawl. "You'll catch your death."

"I have a coat."

"You have a coat and you're not in it." Nesta put the shawl round her shoulders anyway. She had been with Arwen four years and had learned that the position of a lady's maid permitted one to be a tyrant within certain strict limits, and she exercised this to the full. "There's a rumour below that a millionaire's aboard. Mr. Astor. And Mr. Guggenheim, with a — with a lady who is not his wife."

"Nesta."

"I'm only saying what the steward said."

"Then say what the steward said more quietly."

Nesta, seventeen decks of propriety and one pair of boots, subsided. Then she said, in Welsh, which she always did when she was being serious: "Will it be all right, ma'am? In New York."

"Of course it will."

"Only my cousin Rhys says New York is full of Irish."

"Your cousin Rhys is a fool. New York is full of everybody." Arwen pulled the shawl tighter. "You'll have your own room. You'll have Sundays. You'll be able to see the shop windows on Sixth Avenue, which are lit with electricity, and you'll be able to walk in the park, and if anyone says a word to you about your accent you will tell them that your accent is worth four hundred years and theirs is worth about ten minutes."

Nesta considered this. "And will you be happy?"

The tenders were pulling away. Below, on the water, a man was standing up in the bow of the *Traffic* with his hat in his hand and his hair blowing, and he was laughing at nothing at all.

"I shall be married," said Arwen, "in June."

That night she could not sleep, and so at one in the morning she put her coat on over her dressing gown, which was indecent, and went up.

The ship was a different animal at night. The public rooms were shut and shuttered; the corridors had been turned down to a low amber glow; a single steward was asleep sitting up in a chair outside the reading room with his mouth open. But the decks were alive. There was a man somewhere forward coiling rope, and the rope went *whisk, whisk, whisk*, and there was a boy with a bucket of sand, and there was a rhythm under the whole thing, a slow deep pulsing that you stopped hearing after ten seconds and that had been going on so long by now that you could feel it in the soles of your feet when you took your shoes off.

She walked aft along the boat deck, past the lifeboats.

She did not look at them. There was no reason to look at them. They were white and they hung in their davits and they were, she supposed, the sort of thing one was glad of in principle, in the same way one was glad of a fire escape.

At the after end of the deck, near the second-class entrance, two figures were standing with their heads together. Officers — she could see the braid. One was talking, low and fast, and the other was laughing, and then the laughing one said, in a voice that carried in the way that voices carry at night over water:

"— and I told him, look you, if that's your idea of a sounding, I'd sooner trust a farmer with a bit of string."

And the other one said something, and the first one said, "Aye, well. There's tidy and there's *tidy*."

Welsh. Not stage Welsh, not the sing-song of a music hall. Welsh of the sort that came off a hill farm and had been sanded down at sea.

Arwen stopped walking. Her heart did something absurd and entirely physical, a lurch, a stumble, as if she had missed a stair in the dark.

The two officers turned. She saw the braid, the white jackets, the caps; she saw one face briefly, young, surprised, a stranger. The other man had already turned away, going forward at a long-legged stride, and she saw his back and the shape of his shoulders and the way he carried his head, and she thought — no. She did not think it. Her body thought it for her, and her body was a fool, because it had been twelve years and men do not stay the same shape, and half the men in Wales had that walk and half the men in Wales said *look you*.

"Ma'am?" said the young officer. "Are you all right? It's mortal cold up here."

"Perfectly," she said. "Thank you."

She went back to her cabin and lay down in the dark with her heart going like a bird in a box, and told herself that she was a widow of twenty-nine with a wedding in June and that she had imagined it, and she was almost asleep, and then she sat straight up in bed and said, aloud, into the dark:

"*Harold.*"

---

Queenstown was the eleventh of April, and it took an hour and a half, and Arwen missed most of it.

She had slept at last at half past four and woken at ten with her mouth tasting of pennies, and by the time she got on deck the tenders *Ireland* and *America* were already alongside with the last of the mail and the last of the passengers: a hundred and thirteen of them, most of them in steerage, most of them leaving home for the last time — girls in shawls with their hair still wet from being combed, a man with a fiddle case, a boy of about twelve carrying a birdcage with no bird in it.

A woman on the tender was holding up a baby so that someone on the quay could see it. The somebody on the quay was too far away to see it. The woman held it up anyway.

Arwen stood at the rail with her hands on the wood and watched, and thought about her mother, who had died in '09 and who had held up no babies at any point in her life, and who had said to Arwen once, in the kitchen in Blaenau, with the rain coming down the window like a hand, *You'll marry where I tell you and you'll thank me for it in ten years.*

*It's been twelve, Mam,* she thought. *I'll let you know.*

Then the tenders pulled away, and the *Titanic* blew her whistle — three long blasts that went through Arwen's chest like a hand — and the land began to slide, and the last thing she saw of Ireland was a woman on the pier with her apron over her face.

That evening she dined with Mrs. Thorne again, and afterwards they walked the promenade deck in the dark. It was warmer now, the Gulf Stream doing its work. The sea was like a floor of black glass with a shine on it. There was no moon to speak of.

"Tell me something," said Mrs. Thorne. "You've been in a brown study all day. Is it the man in New York?"

"No."

"Then it's a man somewhere else."

Arwen laughed, and it came out wrong. "There isn't one. There was one. There was one a very long time ago, when I was seventeen, and he went to sea, and I married someone else, and that is the whole of it, and it is not even a sad story. It's a very ordinary story. Half the women in Wales have it."

"Half the women in Wales are fools," said Mrs. Thorne. "What was his name?"

"Lowe," said Arwen. "Harold Lowe."

And even saying it, after twelve years, in the dark, on that deck, she felt the ship move under her the way it had moved at Southampton: not a shudder. A pressure. As if a hand had been laid flat on her, and was pushing her, slowly, back.

# II

## The Quarry and the Sea

### Merionethshire · Summer 1900

They came to Barmouth on the second Saturday in July, as they had come every year since Arwen was six, and the arrival was always the same: a fly from the station with the trunks roped on behind, her mother holding her hat, Ifan hanging out of the window like a dog, and her father sitting bolt upright with his hands on the head of his stick, reading the town as if it were a balance sheet.

"Brynhyfryd," he said. "Same house."

"Same house, Rhys," said her mother.

"Same house, same smell of kippers, same woman in the kitchen who can't do a chop without drowning it." He said this with tremendous satisfaction. Rhys Prys complained about his holidays the way other men praised them, and everyone in the family understood that this was his way of being happy, and accommodated it.

Arwen sat with her back to the horse and watched the sea come at her in pieces between the houses. She was seventeen. She had been seventeen for four months and was in that condition of life in which every single thing — a shop sign, a gull, the particular green of a wave — arrived with an unbearable amount of meaning attached to it, and she had no idea what to do with any of it, and it made her want to bite people.

"Look," said Ifan. "Look, there's the bridge. Arwen. Arwen, look."

"I'm looking."

"You're not looking, you're looking at nothing."

"I am looking at the bridge."

The Barmouth bridge went out across the mouth of the Mawddach on its forest of timber trestles, a mile of it, black against the water, and the tide was out so that the sand below it was a shine of wet brown as far as the eye could see, and there were people on it, small as fleas, walking out to the middle for no reason at all except that it was there and it was Sunday tomorrow and they had one day left.

The house was on the hill behind the town, above the chapel, with a garden that grew mostly stones. Arwen had the back room that year, which was the worst room in the house, because Ifan had put in a claim based on the fact that he was twelve and needed air. But the worst room in the house had a window that faced the estuary, and at half past nine that first night, when the light had gone the colour of weak tea, she sat in the window seat with her knees up and watched a boat come down the river with the tide.

It came slowly, under a brown sail, and there was a boy in it, standing, doing something with the sheet, and he was singing — she could hear it, faintly, across two hundred yards of evening, and it was Welsh, and it was not a song she knew.

That was the first time. She did not know it was the first time. She thought it was a boat.

---

The next Wednesday her father hired a boat and a boy to row them out to the bar for the mackerel, because it was what one did, and it cost a shilling and the boy was recommended by the landlord of the Fanny Talbot as a lad who knew the water.

The boy was waiting on the shingle with his cap pushed back and his sleeves rolled and his feet bare, and he was not a boy at all in the way that Arwen had been using the word. He was very brown. He had a nose that had been broken and set by somebody who did not care, and a jaw, and hands like a bundle of tools. He looked at the family in the boat — the father in the stern with his panama, the mother with her parasol, Ifan already trailing a line, and Arwen in the middle with her hands folded like a girl in a painting — and he said, "Right. Nobody stand up. If you stand up, you'll go in, and I'll not be diving after you in this."

"Are you the boy?" said her father.

"I'm the boy," said the boy.

"What's your name?"

"Lowe."

Arwen looked up. He was pushing off with an oar, and the muscles in his forearms moved like something under a rug, and he did not look at her at all, and she felt, with a sudden and total and humiliating clarity, that she had been looked at by every man in Merionethshire that week and had not noticed one of them, and that this one had not looked and she had noticed.

They went out on the ebb with the sail up, and the boy — Lowe — sat in the stern with his hand on the tiller and the sheet round his wrist and his eyes going from the sail to the water to the shore and back, always in that order, like a man counting a rosary.

"Tide turned at three," said her father, expansively, to nobody. "We'll have two hours on the bar and then we'll want to be off it. I know this water. My grandfather kept a sloop at Porthmadog and I was on it before I could walk."

"Tide turned at half past two," said Lowe.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Half past two. It'll be slack by four and running fair by half four." He said it without heat, the way a man says the time of day. "We'll want to be off it by four."

Her father's moustache performed a small manoeuvre. "We shall see," he said.

We saw. At four o'clock the water on the bar began to stand up in little pyramids, and the boat, which had been lying quietly with four lines out and a basket of mackerel in the bottom, began to pull at her anchor like a horse at a gate. And Lowe said nothing at all — that was the beautiful part, he said nothing at all, he simply looked at her father with an expression of infinite patience, and her father said, with enormous dignity, "Well. We'd best be off it, then."

They ran back up the estuary with the wind on their quarter and the sail cracking, and Ifan was screaming with delight, and her mother had her eyes shut, and her father had forgotten to be annoyed and was holding his hat and laughing his real laugh, the one from the bottom of him, which Arwen heard perhaps four times a year. And Arwen sat with her hand on the gunwale and the spray coming over, and she was aware of every inch of her own skin.

At the shingle, when they paid him, the boy said, "The young lad can come out again if he likes. There's a crab to be got off the rocks at low water, and I don't mind."

"Can I?" said Ifan. "Mam. Can I?"

"Not alone," said their mother.

"He won't be alone," said Lowe. "He'll be with me."

And that was how it started: not with Arwen at all, but with Ifan, and a crab, and a shilling.

---

She found excuses for eleven days.

There was the shopping, which could be done at eleven or at four and which she began to do at four. There was the walk along the promenade to the baths, which could be taken in either direction and which she began to take in the direction of the slip. There was the mending for the chapel bazaar, which could be done anywhere, and which she began to do on the wall by the harbour with her straw hat shading her eyes and a book face-down on her knee.

He was working. That was the thing she had not expected, and it changed everything. He was up at five, and he rowed people out to the bar, and he carried luggage up from the station for tuppence a trunk, and he took the fishing parties out, and he cleaned the bottoms of the visitors' boats for a shilling and a bottle of beer, and in the evenings he mended nets for a man named Bevan who had one hand. He was seventeen years old and he had the schedule of a man of forty.

"Where did you learn to sail?" she asked him, on the fourth day.

"Here."

"Who taught you?"

"The water."

"That's not an answer, that's a proverb."

He looked at her then, properly, for the first time, and there was something in his face that she would spend years trying to name and would eventually settle on calling *appraisal* — the look of a man checking the set of a sail, deciding whether it would draw. "My father was a master," he said. "Under sail, in the Baltic trade, and then anything he could get. He went down off the Skerries when I was nine. I remember the man coming up the path to tell my mother. He had his hat in his hands and he couldn't say it." He picked at a knot in the net. "So it's not a proverb. It's just where I learned. Off here, and off him, before that."

"And you'll go to sea."

"I've been to sea. I went at fourteen, on a schooner out of Liverpool, and I've been to the Plate and back twice and to Quebec once and I was in Cardiff most of last winter." He said this evenly. "I'm for a berth in September. I've a man in Liverpool who'll have me."

"September," she said, and heard her own voice go small, and hated it.

"Aye."

Four weeks. She had four weeks, and she had not known she was counting until she counted.

---

The summer had a shape to it, though she did not see the shape until afterwards. It was built out of very small things.

There was the afternoon the rain came in off the bay like a wall and they sheltered in the old bathing hut at the north end, and he told her about the Plate — the River Plate, the brown water, the beef boats, the cold off the Horn — and she told him about the quarry, and about the sound the saws made, and about how when she was small she had thought the slate tips were mountains and had been told they were not, and had cried. And he laughed at that, a real laugh, sudden and rough, and she thought: *I have made him laugh. That is a thing I have done that no one can take off me.*

There was the evening of the fair, when the hurdy-gurdy man came and the whole town was out, and Ifan won a coconut and lost it in the same hour, and Arwen and Harold Lowe walked up the hill behind the town in the dark, not touching, with a foot of air between them the whole way, and he said, "You'll be going back to Blaenau."

"At the end of August."

"It's a terrible place."

"It is not."

"It's a hole in the ground with a town on top of it."

"It's the biggest slate quarry in the world," she said, "and my father owns a third of it, and every roof in London is on my father's slate, and I'll thank you not to call it a hole in the ground."

"Aye," he said, "but you don't like it."

She stopped walking. Nobody had ever said that to her. Nobody in her life had said that to her. She had spent seventeen years being the daughter of Rhys Prys of Blaenau and had never once, in any company, said out loud that she did not like it, and here was a boy with no shoes on saying it on a hill in the dark as if it were the most obvious thing in the world.

"How do you know that?"

"Because you talk about it like a woman talking about a dead husband," he said.

And she laughed, and then she stopped laughing, and then she kissed him.

She had not planned to. She had, in fact, planned in considerable detail not to. She was a chapel-raised girl of seventeen and she knew precisely what happened to girls who kissed boys on hills — she had heard it from the pulpit and from her mother and from the ladies of the sewing circle, and the whole apparatus of it had been set up in her head since she was eleven, and it went off like a mouse-trap the moment her mouth touched his and she found that she did not care, that she had never cared, that the whole apparatus was made of paper.

He went very still. Then his hand came up and rested on her jaw, very lightly, as if he were holding something he had been told not to break, and he kissed her back with a care that was somehow worse than passion, and when he let her go he said, in Welsh, "Duw, help me."

"Don't," she said. "Don't say that."

"I'm not much of a one for chapel," he said. "But that's what I've got."

---

Her mother spoke to her about it once, in the kitchen, in September, when it was already over.

She did not speak about the boy. She spoke about the quarry, which was her way.

"Your father's a good man," said Margaret Prys, with her hands in the flour. "He's a good man and he's a hard man and he thinks he's doing right by you, and he is doing right by you, or he would be if you were a different girl."

"Mam."

"I'm not saying a word against him. I've been married to him twenty-six years and I'd not change a day of it, and if you tell him I said that I'll deny it." She turned the dough. "But I'll tell you a thing about your father and you can do what you like with it. He's never in his life been able to tell the difference between a thing that's valuable and a thing that's worth something. He knows slate. He can look at a piece of slate and tell you what it'll fetch in London. And he looked at me in 1875 and he could not tell you what I'd fetch, and he married me anyway, and he's never once regretted it, and he doesn't know why." She put the dough down. "That's all. That's the whole of what I've got. Kindness is not nothing, *cariad*, but it's not the same as the other thing, and a woman can spend forty years being grateful and be a good wife and die with something still in her that nobody ever got at."

"What's the other thing?"

"I don't know what it's called," said her mother. "I never had it. But I've seen it, and it makes people do foolish things, and it's worth about as much as the slate and about as much as nothing at all, and God help you if you've got it, because there's no living with it and no living without it."

She went back to the bread. That was the end of the conversation, and neither of them ever mentioned it again.

---

She made the heart in the last week of August, sitting on the wall above the harbour with a splinter of slate on her knee and a nail.

The slate she had picked up on the beach at Fairbourne, where the ferry crossed; it was green, the good green, the colour of the sea when the sun is behind a cloud, and it split under her thumbnail into thin sheets that rang. She chose a piece the size of a penny and scratched a heart on it with the nail, badly, twice, until the second one satisfied her, and then she bored a hole through the top of it with the same nail and a stone, and it took her an hour and a half and she broke two nails and her thumb was bleeding by the end.

She threaded it on a bootlace. It was not a beautiful object. It was, in fact, a fairly ugly object, slightly lopsided, with a grey scratch down one side of the heart where the nail had slipped.

She gave it to him on the shingle at ten at night on the twenty-ninth of August, with the tide coming in and the lamps going along the promenade and the smell of somebody frying onions behind them.

"What's this?"

"A heart."

"I can see it's a heart." He turned it over. "Is it worth anything?"

"It's worth a great deal," she said. "It's the biggest slate quarry in the world."

He laughed. Then he stopped laughing and looked at it for a long time, and then he took off his jacket in front of her, which he had never done, and he was wearing a vest and his collarbones stood out and there was a thin white scar on his shoulder that she would never get the story of, and he put the bootlace round his neck and tucked the slate inside his vest so that it lay against his skin.

"There," he said. "Now it's worth something."

And then he said, "I'll come back."

"Don't."

"I'll come back for you, Arwen Prys. Whatever your father says, and he'll say a good deal, I should think. I'll come back when I've a berth and a wage and something to put in front of him." He said it without a flicker. "It might be four years. It might be five. You'll wait."

"Yes," she said.

"Say it properly."

"I'll wait."

---

Her father found out on the second of September, from the chapel.

Not from anyone's malice, exactly, or not only. Mrs. Bevan had seen them on the hill. Mrs. Bevan saw everything on the hill; it was her chief pleasure in life and she had been at it for thirty years, and she reported what she saw to the minister, and the minister, who was a good man and a coward, mentioned it to Rhys Prys after the service in the tone of a man mentioning the weather.

Arwen was in the garden when her father came back. She knew at once. She had been seventeen years in that house and she could read the sound of his boots.

"Who is he?"

"Tad—"

"Who is he? The boy with the boat. Bevan's lad, the rower. What's his name?"

"Lowe. Harold Lowe."

"Lowe." He said it the way he might have said *mud*. "And what is he?"

"He's going to sea. He's been at sea since he was fourteen—"

"What *is* he?" Her father's voice was very quiet. That was the worst of it. Rhys Prys did not shout; he had been raised in a house where shouting was what the poor did, and he had spent forty years becoming a man who never raised his voice, and he could put more into a sentence spoken softly than most men could put into a fist.

"He's a sailor," said Arwen.

"He's a sailor," said her father, "and his mother keeps a lodging house on the front at Barmouth with four rooms and a copper, and his father was a Liverpool man who went down in somebody else's ship with nine men and no insurance, and there is a sister who I'm told is in service in Chester. That's the whole of it. That's the estate. Am I wrong?"

"No."

"Then you'll tell me what in the name of God you think you're doing."

She stood there in the garden with her hands at her sides. Behind her, the kitchen window was open and she could hear her mother's knife stop moving on the board, and then start again, twice as fast.

"I'm going to wait for him," Arwen said.

Her father looked at her for a long moment. Then he did the worst thing he could possibly have done, which was not to be angry. He put his hand on her shoulder and said, very kindly, "My poor girl. You'll be waiting a long time, and you'll do it in this house, and you'll do it with the door shut."

---

Harold Lowe sailed from Liverpool on the eighteenth of September, 1900, bound for the Plate, and he came to Barmouth to say goodbye two days before.

They had an hour. Her father stood at the gate the entire time with his watch in his hand, which was a gesture so petty that Arwen could not even find it in her to be angry; she was too busy trying to memorise the boy's face, and the way he stood, and the crack in his voice when he said her name.

"I'll write," he said. "I'll write from the Plate and I'll write from wherever I go after. My hand's terrible, you'll have to guess at half of it."

"I'll answer."

"You'd better." He tried to smile. "Look after that brother of yours. He's a terror. He'll be a sailor himself, you mark me."

"Harold."

"Don't." He put his hand up. "Don't say a long one. I'm no good at long ones."

"Then I'll say a short one," she said, and took his hand and pressed it flat against her own chest, over her heart, so that he could feel it going. "There. That's the short one."

He went out of the gate and down the hill and did not look back, and her father shut the door and bolted it, and that was the last time she saw him for twelve years.

---

He wrote. She knew he wrote; she learned it later, and the learning of it came to her in the way that certain kinds of news come, sideways, years afterwards, from a cousin at a wedding.

Four letters came to Brynhyfryd in the autumn of 1900 and the winter after, addressed in a large, ugly, determined hand to Miss A. Prys, and four letters went into the fire in the study, and nobody ever told her, and she spent two years believing that a boy she had loved had forgotten her in a fortnight.

She learned the shape of waiting instead. She learned to be looked at by the sons of quarry men and slate merchants and to smile and to say nothing, and she learned what her father's kindness was worth, which was exactly as much as her obedience. In the spring of 1902 a widower of thirty-four came to the house — Emlyn Vaughan, of Vaughan & Sons, Porthmadog, shipowners, coal and slate coastwise, a courteous man with a good laugh and grey at the temples and a house in New York that he was enlarging — and her father said, "There. There's a man," and her mother said, "He's kind, *cariad*, and kindness is not nothing," and Arwen looked at him across the tea things and thought: *he is not the sea, but he is a ship, and a ship will do.*

They married in March 1903. He was kind. He was kind for six years, and then he was in Naples on business in the autumn of 1908 and there was typhoid in the harbour quarter, and he came home in a coffin in the hold of his own line's steamer, which was, she thought afterwards, either a joke in very poor taste or the only fitting end for a man who had spent his life arranging other people's cargoes.

There was a daughter in 1906 who did not breathe.

And there were years in New York in a house with a lift in it, and there was the strike at Penrhyn that her father wrote about for two years as though it were a death in the family, and there was a revival in Wales that she read about in the newspapers and did not go home for, and there was a great deal of bridge.

And then, in April 1912, there was a Wednesday, and a ship that smelled like a piano shop, and a man's back going forward along a dark deck at one in the morning.

She lay in her bed on the *Titanic* and looked at the ceiling and said, to the dark, in Welsh, "You never wrote."

And somewhere above her, on the boat deck, a man who had written four times and had his letters burned went on his rounds with a slate heart under his vest and a whistle in his pocket, and did not know she was aboard, and would have said, if anyone had asked him, that he had put all that behind him long ago.

---

# III

## The Man in the White Jacket

### At sea · Friday, 12 April 1912

In the morning the sea was like something laundered.

Arwen woke at seven to a stripe of sun across the ceiling and the ship moving with that long, easy, unhurried roll that is the whole difference between a vessel and a machine, and lay still for a moment listening to it and thinking, with a strange detachment, about the fact that she had said his name out loud in the dark.

She rang for Nesta and was very brisk.

"Which deck is the promenade?"

"A, ma'am. Or there's the one above with the boats."

"Get out my grey. Not the mourning — the grey with the braid. And the hat with the pheasant."

"The pheasant's a bit much for a morning, ma'am."

"The pheasant," said Arwen, "is exactly right for a morning."

At half past eight she was on A deck with her hands in her muff and the pheasant in the wind, walking aft, and the sea on her left was the colour of a knife blade, and there was nobody about but a steward with a tray and a man in a deck chair asleep with his mouth open under a newspaper that said **BELFAST — LAUNCH OF THE WHITE STAR LINER** in letters four inches high, and a boy of about eleven running the length of the enclosed promenade at a dead sprint for no reason at all, the way boys do, three times, until a steward told him to stop.

Arwen walked. She walked to the after end and turned and walked forward again, past the reading room, past the lounge with its windows like the windows of a conservatory, past the doors that led into the grand staircase, and at the top of the staircase, coming up two at a time, was a steward with a leather satchel and a face like a man carrying bad news.

"Steward."

"Madam."

"What's your name?"

"Penney, madam. Albert Penney."

"Penney. You're my steward. You're the one who brought the water at eleven last night and put the jug where I couldn't reach it."

"I'm sure I'm very sorry, madam—"

"Don't be. It was a test and you failed it and we'll say no more about it." She walked a few steps with him, which confused him. "Penney, how many officers are there on a ship this size?"

"On this ship, madam? Eight, madam. Under the captain."

"Eight. And who are they?"

"The chief officer's Mr. Wilde. Then Mr. Murdoch, who's first officer. Then Mr. Lightoller, and Mr. Pitman, and Mr. Boxhall, and Mr. Lowe, and Mr. Moody. Mr. Moody's the youngest, madam, he's not long turned twenty."

Arwen's heart did something so violent that she had to stop walking and pretend to look at the sea.

"Lowe," she said.

"Mr. Lowe, madam. Fifth officer."

"Fifth."

"Yes, madam."

"Thank you, Penney. Put the jug where I can reach it in future."

She stood at the rail for a long time after he'd gone and let the wind take the pheasant and did not notice whether it did or not.

*Fifth officer,* she thought. *Not a boy in a boat. Not a shilling and a shilling. Fifth Officer of the largest ship in the world, in a white jacket, with eight hundred men under him or whatever the number is, and he has been in the North Atlantic twelve years and has a watch and a whistle and a proper berth, and he would be entirely acceptable to my father now, and my father is four years dead and it makes no difference at all.*

*Which is the whole of it,* she thought. *Which is the whole of it, and I am to be married in June.*

---

She did not find him that morning. She found the gymnasium, where a young instructor in a very tight jacket persuaded her onto a mechanical horse and then, when she was on it, increased the difficulty without asking, so that she came off it in a heap and had to be helped up by a Swede of enormous size who said, "It is nothing, it happens to all the ladies," in a tone of profound condolence.

She found the swimming bath, which was empty and full of blue light and had a marble step at one end, and she stood at the rail and looked down into it and thought about how a person could drown in six inches of water and how she had read that somewhere and had never believed it.

She found Mrs. Thorne in the lounge with the griffon on her lap and a novel face-down on the table.

"You're dressed for hunting," said Mrs. Thorne.

"I'm dressed for a morning."

"You're dressed for a morning the way a general is dressed for a parade." She patted the chair beside her. "Sit down and tell me what's happened."

"Nothing has happened."

"Then something is about to, which is worse." Mrs. Thorne signalled a steward with one finger. "Two cups. And the little cakes, not the big ones; the big ones are a scandal." She turned back. "There are four hundred and twenty-five people in first class on this ship, my dear, and I have been watching them for two days, and I can tell you that at this moment exactly three of them are interesting. One is a man with a wooden leg who says he was at Ladysmith and I believe him. One is a woman whose husband is in an asylum in Connecticut and who is going home to him anyway. And one is you, standing at the rail on A deck at half past eight in the morning with a pheasant in her hat, staring at the officers' quarters like a girl at a bakery."

"There are no windows in the officers' quarters."

"Exactly," said Mrs. Thorne. "That's how I knew."

---

The day went by in that long, luxurious, slightly narcotic way that days go by at sea. Luncheon. A nap. The run posted at noon: **484 miles**, in a steward's neat hand, on a card by the purser's office, and a small crowd around it doing sums. The barber. The Turkish bath, where Arwen was steamed and pummelled by two silent men and came out feeling like a joint of meat that had been treated with respect.

At four o'clock she went up to the boat deck, because that was where the officers were, and because she had run out of subtlety.

The boat deck was the top of the world and it was not a place for passengers at four in the afternoon. It was cold and bright and busy and full of things that had no business in a hotel: coils of rope as thick as a man's wrist, a hatch cover, a winch, a smell of tar and grease and cold iron, an iron ladder going up to somewhere she could not see. Two men were painting a ventilator a colour that was almost the colour it had been. A boy in a white jacket was polishing brass with a rag and a tin of something and the arm of his jacket was black to the elbow.

She walked the length of it. There were sixteen boats, she counted them without meaning to — eight on each side, hanging in their davits, white and empty and covered with canvas, and under the canvas were the shapes of oars and barrels.

A man came round the corner of the officers' quarters at a fast walk and nearly knocked her down.

"*Sorry* — " He caught her elbow, steadied her, and let go. He was young, twenty at most, with a face like a friendly choirboy and a cap too big for him. "I do beg your pardon, madam. I wasn't looking."

"Mr. Moody," she said.

He blinked. "Yes, madam."

"I'm told you're the youngest."

"I'm told that too, madam," he said, with a ruefulness that was entirely charming, "generally by Mr. Lowe."

And there it was — a door opening, so easily, in the middle of a bright cold afternoon, and she walked through it without a moment's hesitation. "Is he about?"

"Mr. Lowe? He's — " Moody's eyes went past her, along the deck, and something in his face changed very slightly, the way a young man's face changes when he remembers he is on duty. "He's coming now, madam."

She turned.

---

He came along the deck in the light with the wind taking the tail of his jacket, and he was not looking at her; he was looking at a length of rope in his hand as if it had personally offended him, and he was saying something to the man beside him, and then he looked up.

He stopped.

It was not a dramatic stopping. There was no stumble, no hand to the heart. He simply stopped, the way a man stops when he has walked into a glass door, and the man beside him went on three paces before he noticed, and turned, and looked, and looked away again with great discretion.

"Mr. Lowe," she said.

"Madam."

The word went through her like a needle. *Madam.*

"Mr. Moody," said Harold Lowe, without taking his eyes off her, "go and see if the fourth officer's got those figures for the noon run. And take your time about it."

"Sir," said Moody, and went, and did not look back, and had the decency to go a long way before he started running.

They stood facing each other on the boat deck of the largest ship in the world, twelve years and four months after a shingle at Barmouth, and the wind was doing what wind does, and neither of them said anything at all.

"You're brown," she said, at last, idiotically.

"I've been in the West Indies." His voice was exactly the same. That was the thing that undid her — twelve years and the voice was exactly the same, with the same flat Merioneth vowels and the same way of putting a full stop at the end of a sentence like a man hammering in a nail. "Arwen."

"Harold."

"I'd heard you were married."

"I was. He died. Four years ago. Typhoid." She heard herself listing it like a passenger manifest and could not stop. "I'm going to New York. I live there. I'm — I'm going home."

"To be married," he said.

She looked at him sharply. "How did you know that?"

"Because you've a ring on your right hand with a stone in it the size of a pea and a band on your left with no stone at all, and a woman wearing a dead husband's ring is either a widow in mourning or a widow in a hurry, and you're not in mourning." He said it flatly, without cruelty. "And because you said 'going home' like a woman reading out a sentence somebody else wrote."

"That's a hateful thing to say."

"Aye," he agreed. "I've been told."

"You've not changed at all."

"I've changed a great deal. I've a watch of my own and a berth on the biggest ship afloat and a temper that's cost me two ships, and I'm thirty years old in November and I've nobody to blame for anything any more." He looked at her, and the flatness went out of him all at once, and what was underneath was so raw that she had to look away. "But not about this. About this I've not changed at all, God help me."

"Harold—"

"Not here." He glanced along the deck; a man in a bowler hat was coming out of the smoking room door with a cigar, and a steward was crossing with a tray, and the whole ship was watching, always, everywhere, all the time. "Not here. There's a — " He rubbed his jaw. "I've the middle watch the night. Till four. After that I'm off till noon." He said it like a man laying out a chart. "There's a place under the after bridge, port side, where the tarpaulin's up. Nobody goes there. Six o'clock. Before the passengers are about."

"Six in the morning."

"Six in the morning." He turned to go, and then turned back, and his hand went to his collar, and he pulled out a bootlace with something on it.

The slate heart had worn smooth. The edges were round as a pebble, and the grey scratch where her nail had slipped was still there, and the lace it hung on had been replaced at least twice, and it lay in his brown palm in the sunlight like a piece of the sea.

"Twelve years," he said.

"Twelve years," she said.

Then he put it back inside his shirt and went away forward along the deck, and Arwen stood where she was, with her hands at her sides, in the wind, and did not cry, and would like it recorded that she did not cry, although she was not asked and there was no one there to see.
---

She went down to the lounge at half past five because her hands were shaking and she wanted to be in a room with other people in it.

The lounge was full. It was always full at half past five: the bridge players and the tea drinkers and the women with novels and the men who had discovered that a ship was an excellent place to avoid their wives by pretending to be busy. Arwen took a chair by the window with her back to the room and pretended to read, and after ten minutes she gave it up and simply sat.

Behind her, three men were talking about the ship.

"— forty-six thousand tons," one of them was saying. "Forty-six thousand gross. You could put the *Mauretania* inside her and have room for a conservatory."

"And the compartments. Sixteen. Any four of them flooded and she'd still float."

"Any *six*, I'm told."

"Six? That's not possible."

"It's in the *Shipbuilder*, my dear fellow. I read it in the train. Fifteen bulkheads, sixteen compartments, and she's designed so that any two adjacent compartments can be flooded and she'll still steam. Any two. That's the principle of the thing." There was the sound of a match. "And she's got a double bottom and eleven watertight doors that close by pressing a button on the bridge, and a man can stand on the bridge and shut the whole ship up like a house at night."

"Well," said the first man. "There you are, then."

"Of course," said the third, who had not spoken before and who had a dry voice, "there's the other business."

"What other business?"

"The boats."

"What about the boats?"

"There aren't enough of them. There's sixteen boats and four collapsibles and they'll hold about a thousand and a bit between them, and there's two and a half thousand people on this ship counting the crew. It's the Board of Trade scale. They go by tonnage, not by people; a ship this size is required to carry boats for a thousand and it does, and there's not a liner afloat that carries more." A pause. "It's the law. It's the same on the *Mauretania*. It's the same on every ship you've ever crossed in."

There was a silence of a particular quality — the silence of three comfortable men deciding, without any of them saying it, that the subject was not a comfortable one.

"Well," said the first man, at last, "it's not as if she's going to sink."

"No," said the dry man. "It's not as if she's going to sink."

They laughed. Arwen heard them laugh, three men in a lounge at half past five in the afternoon, and she looked out of the window at the flat grey sea and thought about the word *unsinkable*, and how much she disliked it, and how she could not have said why.

Her husband had used that word.

Emlyn Vaughan had used it about the *Lusitania* in 1907, standing on the deck of a much smaller ship in the Mersey with his hat in his hand and the wind coming up the river, and he had said, "There's the future, Arwen. Forty thousand tons and a turbine and they say she'll be unsinkable." And she had said, "Nothing is unsinkable," and he had laughed and said, "You're a gloomy woman," and she had said, "I'm a quarryman's daughter. We build roofs for people and then we watch them fall in."

That was a thing she had said, once, on a cold day in Liverpool, five years before he died, and she had not thought of it since, and now she thought of it in the lounge of the *Titanic* and put her hand over her mouth.

---

She wrote a paragraph of a letter to Cedric and it went badly.

*My dear Cedric, — I am four days out and the weather has been fine. I find I am not the sailor I was. There is a man on board who was at Barmouth in the summer I was seventeen, and I have not thought of him in years, and I find that I have thought of him every day, and I do not know what to do with that, and I am telling you because you are the only person in the world I could tell it to and be sure of being treated kindly.*

She read it back and then tore it into four pieces and put the pieces in her pocket, because it was not true, or it was true in the wrong way, or it was true in a way that no letter could carry, and because there was a woman two chairs away writing steadily and happily and without any difficulty at all.

At seven she went down to dress for dinner and found Nesta laying out the green silk with an air of great purpose.

"The green?"

"The green," said Nesta. "You've a colour in your face tonight."

"Have I?"

"You have. You've had it since Thursday." Nesta shook out the skirt. "And you've eaten a whole breakfast and a whole luncheon and you've not once asked me what the kitchen was doing, which you always do when you're low, so I'll say nothing about it and neither will you."

Arwen looked at her in the glass.

"Nesta."

"Ma'am."

"Thank you."

"Don't," said Nesta, "or I shall cry and then we'll both be a mess and the green will be no good to anybody."

---

She went down to dinner and could not eat.

"What's the matter with you?" said Mrs. Thorne, who had invited herself to Arwen's table on the first night and had not been dislodged since.

"Nothing."

"You've moved your peas into a pattern."

Arwen looked at her plate. The peas were arranged in a circle.

"I'm going to be very foolish at six o'clock tomorrow morning," she said.

Mrs. Thorne put down her fork. "Thank God," she said. "I was beginning to think I'd have to arrange it myself."

"Don't. Don't make it a joke."

"I'm not making a joke. I'm sixty years old, or near enough, and I've buried a husband I loved and I've sat in a house in Beacon Street for six years listening to the clock, and I can tell you that foolishness at six in the morning is the only thing on earth worth getting up for." She leaned forward. "Who is he?"

"Nobody. He's the fifth officer."

"The Welshman."

Arwen looked up. "How—"

"Because on Wednesday night you told me about a boy called Lowe who went to sea, and on Thursday morning you asked my cabin steward a great many questions about the officers, and last night at eleven o'clock you were not in your bed, and this afternoon you came down from the boat deck with your hat on back to front." Mrs. Thorne picked up her fork again. "My dear. I have been a widow in a large house for six years. I am the best detective in America."

At the next table a large man in evening dress was telling a story about a horse, and the people around him were laughing in the immoderate way of people who have paid for their dinner and intend to be amused by it, and beyond him the window was a black square with the ship's lights in it, going by.

"He was my first," said Arwen. "That's all. He was my first and I was seventeen and my father burned his letters, only I didn't know about the letters until years later, and I married a good man and was a good wife to him and I have been a widow for four years and I am engaged to be married in June to a good man in New York who has never once raised his voice or surprised me, and I am not unhappy, and I am not happy, and I shall be thirty next spring, and until Tuesday I had not thought about Harold Lowe in — " She stopped.

"Go on."

"I thought about him every day," said Arwen. "I thought about him every single day for twelve years and I did not know I was doing it. It was like breathing."

Mrs. Thorne was silent for a moment. Then she said, "What is the fiancé's name?"

"Cedric. Cedric Ashby."

"Does he deserve this?"

"He deserves better than this."

"Then he is a fool," said Mrs. Thorne, "and foolishness in a husband is incurable," and she signalled for the wine.

---

At half past eleven Arwen went out on the promenade deck alone, because she could not lie down.

It was warmer than it had been. The sea was black and moving and there was a long low swell out of the southwest that made the ship roll a hand's breadth, no more, and each time she rolled the water along her side made a small private noise, like a door closing in another room.

Two men were standing at the rail further forward, talking. One of them was small and grey and had his hands behind his back, and the other was taller, and they were talking in the low, easy way of men who have talked to each other every day for years.

" — she's a good ship," the smaller one was saying. "She'll do very well. It's a pity about the — "

And then they moved away, and she lost it, and she stood there in the dark for a while with her hands on the cold rail, and thought about the fact that in seven hours she would be standing under the after bridge with a man who had worn a piece of her father's slate against his chest for twelve years.

When she went in, the clocks had been put back forty minutes, and a card in the lift said so, so that the passengers might have their sleep.

She did not sleep.

---

# IV

## A Walk Before Breakfast

### At sea · Saturday, 13 April 1912

She dressed herself in the dark, which took three times as long as it should have and produced a result that Nesta would have wept over.

The grey dress had a row of eleven hooks down the back. Arwen got nine of them and left two, and put the coat over the top, and pinned her hat by feel, and looked at herself in the glass and saw a woman of thirty with her hair badly done and her eyes too bright, and thought: *well. There you are.*

Nesta stirred in the outer cabin. "Ma'am?"

"Sleep."

"It's not six."

"It's not six," Arwen agreed, and went out.

The corridor was lit for night. She went up the broad staircase past the clock with the two bronze figures on it, and out through the doors onto the boat deck into a cold that took her breath like a hand.

It was not dark. That was the first surprise. In the north-east, low down, there was a smear of grey-green like a bruise going yellow, and the sea had begun to come up out of the dark in pieces, and the whole ship was a set of black shapes against a lighter black. Somewhere aft, a man was singing — not a song, just two notes, over and over, the way men sing when they are working and cold.

He was where he said he would be. Under the after bridge, port side, in the lee of a tarpaulin, with his cap off and his hair standing up and two tin cups in his hands.

"Morning," he said.

"Good morning."

"Here." He gave her one of the cups. It was coffee, foul, stewed, with sugar in it and something else — condensed milk, she thought — and it was so hot that it hurt her hands through the tin, and it was, without any question, the best thing she had ever been given.

"Where did you get this?"

"The galley. I've a friend in the galley." He drank from his own cup. "He thinks I've a woman in a cabin. He's not wrong about the woman, so I let him think the rest."

"Harold."

"It's a joke. Sit down. There's a coil of rope and it's dry."

She sat on the coil of rope. He sat on the deck with his back against the tarpaulin and his long legs out in front of him, and for a while they drank their coffee and said nothing, and the sky went from bruise to pearl, and the ship's bow, away forward, made its long hiss into the dark.

"I wrote to you," he said.

"I know. I found out — I found out years afterwards. A cousin. She said there were letters, four of them, and that my father—"

"He burned them."

"He burned them," she said. "I never saw one. I thought—" She stopped and made herself say it. "I thought you'd forgotten me in a fortnight. I thought you'd got to the Plate and seen the world and thought, well, that was a bit of a summer, and I was a girl on a wall with a book."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "The first one I wrote off Cardiff in a gale with the ink going all over. I wrote it four times. I've never been so careful with a thing in my life and I've never been so careful since." He turned the cup round in his hands. "I said, I've a berth on the *Marquis* and it's two pound ten and my board, and I'll be at Barry in the spring, and will she meet me at the station. That was the first one."

"Harold."

"The second one was worse. I'd had a bad passage and I'd got into a row with the mate and I was in a foul mood and I wrote like a fool, and I've thought about that letter for twelve years, wondering what she'd have made of it." He laughed, shortly. "Nothing good, I should think."

"Harold."

"And then I stopped, because a man can only write into a hole in the ground so many times."

"I never got them." She put her cup down on the deck. Her hands were shaking and she did not want him to see, so she put them in her lap, and then she did not care whether he saw, so she took them out again. "Twelve years. I've been angry with you for twelve years."

"I know."

"You don't know. I've been *angry* with you. In New York, in the house with the lift in it, with a good man sitting across the table being kind to me, I have sat there and thought about you and been angry in a way that—" She stopped.

"That what?"

"That I was ashamed of," she said. "That's the truth of it. I was ashamed of how much I minded. I thought, this is ridiculous, this is a girl's nonsense, I am a married woman, and I would put it away and it would come back. It always came back."

He nodded slowly, as if she were telling him about the weather in a port he had not visited. "Aye," he said. "It's like that."

"Is it?"

"It's like having a stone in your boot that you can't get at." He looked up at the paling sky. "I've been in thirteen ships. I've been to the Plate and the Black Sea and the West Indies and I've been ashore in places where the women wear nothing but a bit of cloth and I've been ashore in places where they wear more than you do. And every blessed one of them, the first week, I'd think: she'd like this. She'd have something to say about this." He shrugged. "It's a stupid thing. It's not even a sad thing. It's just a stone in your boot."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Tell me about the ships. Not the ports. The ships."

"What d'you want to know?"

"Everything. I've never once known what it is you actually do."

He thought about it. He was not a man who had ever been asked to explain his own life, and he did it the way he did everything, which was by starting in the middle.

"The *Marquis* was a barque," he said. "Three masts, square rig, eighteen hundred tons. I was eighteen and I was in the starboard watch and I'd a mate called Crouch who was forty and had a wife in Cardiff and a mouth like a sewer. We went out in a gale in October and we were eleven days getting round Ushant and I was sick for four of them and Crouch stood over me and laughed and then gave me half his cocoa." He turned the tin cup round. "There was a lad called Pritchard in that ship. Seventeen. He'd run away from a farm in Cardigan. He could not learn the ropes; it wasn't laziness, he was stupid, he was honest-to-God stupid, he could not learn a knot to save his life. And one night off the Plate we were taking in the topsails in a squall and he went out on the yard and he went off it."

Arwen said nothing.

"We were nine hours finding him," said Harold Lowe. "Nine hours, hove to, with the boats out, and it was dark and there was a sea running and we never found anything at all. And when we gave it up the captain said a prayer and the men went below and Crouch sat down on a hatch cover and cried like a child, and I stood there and thought: *he was stupid, and now he's dead, and if I'd been a better hand at teaching him the knot he might have held on.*" He looked at her. "That's the whole of it. That's the whole of what I do. I keep a ship and I keep the men in her and I try to bring them home, and sometimes I can't, and the ones I can't, I carry."

"How many?"

"Don't ask me that."

"I'm asking."

"Four," he said. "In twelve years. Four that were mine to keep and that I lost. Pritchard, and a man called Osei who went down a hold on the *Marquis* — that was nobody's fault, the ladder was rotten — and a fireman on the *Sardinian* who went over the side in the Mersey at three in the morning and I've never been sure to this day whether he slipped or whether he didn't, and a boy on the *Belgic* in '09 who I pulled out of the water myself and who died in my arms on the deck, and I was twenty-six years old and I sat down on the deck beside him and couldn't get up." He said it evenly, without any drama, the way a man reads out a list of stores. "There'll be more. That's the trade."

"And you'd not do anything else."

"I'd not do anything else." He looked up. "You'll think that's a terrible thing to say, a man sitting in the cold at six in the morning saying he'd not do anything else when it's killed four men out from under him. But it's the truth of me. I've a hand for it. I've been in a boat in a sea that would take your legs off and I've not been afraid, or I've been afraid after, which is the same thing. And when I'm on a bridge in a black night with a light somewhere out to starboard and the ship under me doing what I tell her, I'm — " He stopped, and hunted for the word, and could not find it, and made a gesture with his hand. "I'm at home. That's all. It's a poor sort of home. But it's mine."

The sky was going gold at the edge now, and the sea had come up out of the dark all at once, and it was grey and steady and there was a line of cloud away to the east that would burn off by eight, and the whole enormous ship was coming awake: a door banging somewhere below, a man coughing, the smell of bread from a ventilator, the sound of the stokers' watch coming up through a hatch, boots and voices and one man laughing like a dog barking.

"The sun'll be up in a minute," he said. "And then I'll have to go. And I want to say the rest of it before I do, because I'll lose my nerve, and I'm not a man who loses his nerve."

"Say it, then."

"You're another man's."

"I'm not another man's. I'm my own, more or less, and I've said yes to a man in New York and there's a ring on my finger and no date in the church yet—"

"There's a ring on your finger," he said, "and I'll not be a man who comes in the side door. I've seen it done. I've seen it done to a shipmate of mine in Valparaiso and it took four years off him." He held up a hand before she could speak. "I'll not do it. I'll not have you in a hotel in Southampton on a Tuesday, and me going out to the West Indies, and letters that somebody else burns. If it's to be done it'll be done in daylight with my name on it and your father's slate in my pocket, and if it can't be done that way then I'd sooner it wasn't done at all, because I've enough to carry without that."

Arwen said nothing. The sun came up out of the sea.

It came up fast, the way it does, and the whole deck went from grey to amber in about ninety seconds, and the tarpaulin lit up and the brass on the winch lit up and Harold Lowe's face lit up, and she saw that he had been crying, which he had managed entirely without her noticing, which was, she thought, so exactly like him that she could have laughed.

"Right," he said, in a completely different voice. "That's the sun. I've to go."

"Harold."

"Two things." He stood up and brushed his trousers. "The first is that I'll not seek you out. I'll not be dodging round after a first-class lady on my own ship, it's not decent and it's not fair to the other officers, and if the chief got wind of it I'd be off this ship at New York and glad to be off it." He looked down at her. "So if you want me, you'll have to say so. Plain. Not a hint, not a look across the room, not a bit of Welsh so the Americans can't follow. Plain."

"All right."

"The second is that if you don't say it — if you go to New York and marry your Mr. Ashby and have a good life — I'll not think ill of you. I want that understood." He said it very clearly, as if he were reading it off a card. "You've been a widow four years and you've a right to a quiet house and a man who never raises his voice. God knows you've had enough of the other kind, if I know anything about you at all, and I do. So I'll not be a millstone. I'll think you did the sensible thing."

"And what will you do?"

He looked out at the sea, and the light was on it now, and the ship was going through it at twenty-two knots and making a sound like a long breath.

"Me?" he said. "I'll go to sea."

---

By noon the card by the purser's office said **519 miles**, and there was a small crowd around it again, and a man in the smoking room was explaining to anyone who would listen that she would be in New York on Wednesday morning at this rate, and that the Blue Riband was a matter of arithmetic.

Arwen sat in the lounge and tried to read *The Wings of the Dove* and read the same paragraph six times.

"You're doing it again," said Mrs. Thorne.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at nothing with your mouth moving."

"I'm not moving my mouth."

"You are. You look like a woman saying the multiplication table." Mrs. Thorne lowered her novel. "Well? Six o'clock?"

"Six o'clock."

"And?"

"And *nothing*, Lavinia."

"That is not a word that means anything."

Arwen closed her book. "He says he won't come between me and Cedric. He says if I want him I have to say so plainly, and if I don't, he'll think I did the sensible thing, and he'll go to sea." She heard her own voice go flat and hard. "He was extremely reasonable. He was so reasonable I could have hit him with the ashtray."

"Ah," said Mrs. Thorne. "Pride."

"It's not pride."

"It's always pride. It's the only thing men have that they can't be reasoned out of." She picked up her novel again. "What are you going to do?"

"I don't know."

"You do."

"I don't."

"My dear." Mrs. Thorne looked at her over the top of the book. "You didn't get up at half past five in the morning to tell a man you'd made up your mind to marry somebody else."

---

The cold came on Saturday afternoon, and it came on fast.

At two o'clock the promenade was full of people in steamer chairs with rugs over their knees, and by four the deck was empty, and the stewards had put up the glass screens, and the wind had gone round into the northwest, and the temperature had dropped in a way that made the ship's officers look at each other and say nothing.

Arwen went up to the boat deck at half past four because Mrs. Thorne had said it was the only place on the ship where a person could be alone with a thought, and found that it was not, because there were three men in front of the officers' quarters doing something to a collapsible boat, and one of them was Harold Lowe, in his greatcoat, with his cap pulled down.

He looked up. He did not smile. He looked at her for exactly as long as a man can look at a woman without it being noticed, and then he looked down at the boat again.

She went and stood by the rail fifty feet away and looked at the sea, and after a while he came over, wiping his hands on a rag, and stood beside her, a good four feet off, and looked at the sea too.

"It's turning cold," he said.

"I noticed."

"Tomorrow'll be worse. There's ice about."

"Ice."

"Not here. Well south of here." He nodded at the water. "We've had reports all day. The *Caronia*, the *Baltic*, a German boat. There's a field of it off the Grand Banks, and the current brings the bergs down into the lane, and there's no moon to speak of this week, so a man has to keep his eyes open." He said it the way a man says there might be rain. "The captain's taken her a bit south. We'll be all right."

"Are we going very fast?"

"Twenty-two knots. Near enough." He glanced at her. "Why?"

"Because a woman in the lounge this afternoon said we could go faster and I wondered why we didn't."

"Because at twenty-two knots you can stop her in about half a mile, and at twenty-four you can't stop her in time to do anything but say a prayer." He shrugged. "And because it costs coal, and Mr. Ismay is on board, and Mr. Ismay has a great many opinions about coal." He said this last with the precise flatness of a man who has decided not to say the rest of it.

They stood in silence. The wind took the edge of the rag he was holding and flapped it.

"Harold."

"Don't." He said it quickly, quietly. "Not out here. There's a man in the crow's nest can see the whole of this deck and there's another one on the bridge." He folded the rag. "Tomorrow's Sunday. There's service at half past ten in the saloon and I'm not on watch till six in the evening. If you want to speak to me, speak to me tomorrow, and I'll find a way to be somewhere." He turned. "I've to go."

"Harold—"

But he was already walking, and a man was calling him from the front of the boat deck, and he raised a hand and went, and that was that.

---

Nesta was waiting for her in the cabin with her arms folded, which was a bad sign.

"What?"

"Nothing, ma'am."

"That's your nothing face. You've had your nothing face since Thursday."

"I don't have a nothing face."

"You've got a face like a chapel on a wet Tuesday." Nesta picked up a hairbrush and put it down again. "Mrs. Thorne's maid says you were up on the boat deck before six this morning talking to an officer."

"Mrs. Thorne's maid wants a smack."

"Mrs. Thorne's maid is forty and she's been in service since she was twelve and she's kind, and she was worried about you, and I was worried about you, and there's talk below in the first-class pantry which I won't repeat because it's beneath me." Nesta's chin came up. "You're to be married in June."

"I know when I'm to be married."

"To a man who's never been anything but decent to you, and my cousin Rhys says in New York a woman in your position—"

"Nesta." Arwen sat down on the bed. "Sit down. No — sit down, I'm not going to have a conversation with your apron."

Nesta sat, very upright, on the edge of a chair.

"His name is Harold Lowe," said Arwen, "and he's the fifth officer of this ship, and I knew him when I was seventeen in Barmouth, and he asked me to wait for him and I said I would, and my father burned his letters and I married Mr. Vaughan instead." She said it without any drama at all, because she had discovered that morning that the drama had gone out of it and left something harder in its place. "And that's the whole of it. There's nothing improper. I've been up on a boat deck in the cold at six in the morning with a cup of coffee and a man I've not seen in twelve years, and if that's a scandal then the world has run mad."

Nesta was silent for a long moment.

"Did he keep the heart?" she said.

Arwen stared at her.

"The bit of slate," said Nesta, patiently, as to a child. "You told me about it when I first came to you, the second week, when you'd had the sherry. You said you'd made a heart out of a bit of slate and given it to a boy and he'd gone to sea and you'd never heard from him again. You said it was the stupidest thing you'd ever done and you'd do it again tomorrow." She smoothed her apron. "Did he keep it?"

"Yes."

"Then he's not a fool," said Nesta, "whatever else he is," and got up and went to run her a bath.

---

That night the clocks went back forty-seven minutes, and Arwen lay in the dark and listened to the ship, and thought about plain speaking.

It was such a small thing, in the end. Three words. She had said them to a boy on a hill in the dark when she was seventeen and had not had to think about them at all, and now she was twenty-nine and would be thirty in the spring, and she lay in a bed the size of a pony trap on the largest ship in the world and rehearsed a sentence the way she had once rehearsed the collects for the chapel.

*I want you. I've always wanted you. I'll go to New York and I'll tell Cedric the truth and I'll come back.*

Or: *I'll not marry him. I don't know what happens next. I'll not marry him.*

Or, simplest, worst, most true: *Harold. I've been angry with you for twelve years and I'd rather be angry with you than be happy with anyone else.*

She turned over. The ship rolled a hand's breadth and came back.

At the bottom of the trunk, wrapped in a chemise she never wore, there was a sheet of notepaper with the White Star crest on it, and a pen, and a bottle of ink that she had bought in Southampton and had not yet opened. She had intended to write to Cedric on the first night and had not. She had intended to write on the second night and had not.

On Sunday, she decided, she would write to him. And then she would go up to the boat deck at six o'clock, and she would find Harold Lowe, and she would say it plain.

She fell asleep at last a little after one, with the cold coming down outside the hull like a hand, and she dreamed — she was to remember this afterwards, and to be embarrassed by it, and to think about it for the rest of her life — she dreamed of the quarry at Blaenau, and of the sound the saws made, and of a boy rowing a brown-sailed boat down the estuary with the tide.

# V

## The Last Quiet Sunday

### At sea · Sunday, 14 April 1912

She wrote to Cedric at half past eight in the morning, at the little satinwood writing desk in the lounge, with the sea going by the window like a bolt of grey silk and a steward moving quietly about with a feather duster.

*My dear Cedric, —*

*I am writing this in the lounge of the Titanic, which is the most extraordinary room I have ever sat in, and I am sorry to say that I have not been able to stop thinking about the plasterwork. It is imitation Grinling Gibbons, or so the man next to me at dinner says, though he says it in a tone that suggests he would have preferred the real thing and expects me to agree.*

She stopped. She looked at it. She crossed it out.

*My dear Cedric, —*

*I hope this finds you well and that the business with the Boston people has gone as you wished. We are four days out and the weather has been very fine until this morning, when it turned cold. I am quite well. Mrs. Thorne, whom I have mentioned, is a widow from Boston and very good company, and I have won eleven shillings at bridge, which I intend to spend on something entirely frivolous.*

She stopped. She looked at that too, and thought about how a woman could write a whole letter in which every sentence was true and the whole of it was a lie.

She took a third sheet.

*Cedric, —*

*I have been thinking about June.*

She got that far and then sat with the pen in her hand for four minutes while the ship went on at twenty-two knots through the cold, and the steward came and went, and a woman at the next desk wrote a letter to her daughter in Iowa and cried quietly into her handkerchief without ever stopping writing.

Then she folded the third sheet in half and put it in her pocket, and took the second sheet and sealed it, and addressed it to Mr. Cedric Ashby, No. 114 Madison Avenue, New York, and did not put a stamp on it.

---

The service was at half past ten in the first-class dining saloon, and it was, Arwen thought, the strangest church she had ever been in: the long tables taken away, the chairs set out in rows, the great glass dome overhead with the grey morning coming through it, and at the front, where the sideboard with the silver usually stood, a small table with a white cloth and a prayer book on it and a captain.

He read well. That was the thing that surprised her. Captain Smith was a big grey man with a beard and a face like a well-fed lion, and she had expected the sort of service that captains give — the lessons bumbled, the hymn announced twice — and instead he read the lesson in a low, clear, unhurried voice, the way a man reads who has read it four hundred times at sea and has come to his own terms with it.

"...and God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away."

The chairs were full. There were perhaps two hundred people in the room, and the ship's orchestra played, and a woman two rows ahead of Arwen sang the hymn very loudly and very badly and with total conviction, and when they came to "O God, our help in ages past," the whole room stood up and sang it, and Arwen found that she was crying, quietly, without any warning, in a room full of strangers, on a Sunday morning, four days out of Southampton.

Mrs. Thorne put a hand on her arm and left it there.

Coming out, she found herself beside the captain.

He was standing at the foot of the stair with his hands behind his back, greeting people as they left, and it was being done very well — he had a word for the women and a hand on the shoulder for the men and he remembered names, which she thought afterwards was the mark of the man more than anything else that was said about him.

"Mrs. Vaughan," he said.

"Captain."

"You're the Welsh lady. Mr. Ismay pointed you out." He said it with a small dry smile, as though Mr. Ismay's habits were a known quantity. "I'm told you've crossed eleven times."

"Eleven."

"Then you'll know more about the North Atlantic than half my officers." He looked out at the grey sea beyond the glass. "You'll have noticed the cold."

"I've noticed it."

"We'll have a fine day tomorrow," said Captain Smith, "and a flat sea, and I shouldn't wonder if you're in New York on Wednesday night. It's a good ship, Mrs. Vaughan. She's the best of them and I've had thirty-eight years of them." He put his hand out. "Go and get warm."

She shook his hand. It was a big, dry, warm hand and it was entirely steady.

"Thank you, Captain," she said.

That was the whole of their conversation, and she thought about it for the rest of her life, and what she thought about it was this: that he had said *she's the best of them and I've had thirty-eight years of them*, and he had said it with his hand behind his back on a Sunday morning in the middle of the Atlantic, and four days later there was a man on a pier in New York selling photographs of him for a nickel.

---

Afterwards, on the stair, Mrs. Thorne said, "Well. That's the last of the religion for the week."

"You're a heathen."

"I'm a Unitarian, which is worse, and don't tell anyone." She took Arwen's elbow. "Come and walk. It's too cold for the deck, we'll walk the promenade and you'll tell me what's in that letter you didn't post."

"How do you know I didn't post it?"

"Because it's in your pocket, and because you've got a face like a woman who's swallowed a plum stone." Mrs. Thorne steered her past the lift. "I'm not going to pry. I'm going to say one thing and then I'll leave it alone. Are you going to speak to him today?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"Tonight. He's on watch from six till ten and then he's off." Arwen's voice was steady and she was proud of it. "I shall say it tonight."

"Good," said Mrs. Thorne. "Say it before dinner. Things said after dinner have a way of being unsaid by breakfast."

---

The day had a quality to it that Arwen noticed even at the time, and remembered afterwards with a kind of horror.

It was too still. The wind had dropped away to nothing by eleven o'clock, and the sea had gone flat and glassy and the colour of a gun barrel, and the ship went through it without any motion at all — none, not a roll, not a shiver — so that if you shut your eyes you could not have told that you were at sea. People remarked on it at luncheon. People remarked on it all day, in the way that people remark on a dog that has been quiet too long.

The cold was the other thing. The thermometer on the port side of the promenade read thirty-three degrees at noon and did not get above it. The stewards put up every screen. The deck chairs were stacked and lashed. The ship's carpenter was sent round to check the freshwater tanks, which had a habit of freezing at the pipes, and there was a small drama at two o'clock when the ship's bugler could not get a note out of his instrument and had to be fetched a cup of hot water.

At luncheon Mr. Howard Deveaux, of Philadelphia, who had made his money in anthracite and had never quite got over it, told the table that in his opinion the cold was a scandal and that he had paid a great deal of money for a great deal of sun and had received, instead, the climate of his own coal cellar.

"It's the ice," said his wife. "The steward told me there's ice."

"There's always ice," said Mr. Deveaux. "It's the North Atlantic. It's ice all the way from here to Halifax and back again. You don't cross the Atlantic in April for the weather, my dear, you cross it for the *arrival*."

"Mr. Deveaux," said Arwen, "have you crossed often?"

"Eleven times, madam, and I have been late nine of them."

"And the other two?"

"The other two I was dead," said Mr. Deveaux, with immense satisfaction, and laughed until he had to wipe his eyes.

At the far end of the table, a young woman in half-mourning — Mrs. Ellery, whose husband was in the shipping business in Cincinnati — was trying to persuade a two-year-old to eat a potato while a baby slept in a shawl on the chair beside her. The two-year-old won.

---

In the afternoon she sat with the children.

It happened without any decision on her part. She had gone into the lounge at two o'clock with the intention of reading, and the only chair with a clear light was next to Mrs. Ellery, and Mrs. Ellery had the look of a woman who had been on her own with two infants for four days and would have sold her soul for an hour of adult conversation, and so Arwen sat down, and the baby was put into her arms within ninety seconds, and that was the end of the reading.

"His name's John," said Mrs. Ellery. "After my father. The other one's Elizabeth and she's a terror."

"She's beautiful."

"She's a terror. She put a spoon down the lavatory on Friday and the steward had to be fetched and he was very kind about it and I cried." Mrs. Ellery had a soft, quick, confiding way of speaking, like a girl at school. "You're not shocked."

"Nothing shocks a woman who has been married," said Arwen. "Where's your husband?"

"New York. He went on ahead in March, on the *Oceanic*, to find us a house. He's in — " She frowned. "It's a business with an engine. He sells engines. It's very good, he says, and there's a lot of money in it, and I don't understand one word of it and I never have." She smoothed the toddler's hair. "He'll be at the pier. He's been writing every day. He writes a great deal better than he talks."

"And you're going to Cincinnati?"

"Mr. Ellery says Cincinnati is the coming place. He says in twenty years it'll be the finest city in America." She smiled. "He says that about everywhere he's ever been. He said it about Scarborough."

Arwen laughed, and the baby woke up and made a small complaining noise, and she put him up against her shoulder and patted his back the way she had learned to do it in a room in Porthmadog in 1906 for four days, and which she had not done since, and which her hands remembered entirely without her permission.

"You've had one," said Mrs. Ellery.

"I had a daughter. She was born in the May and she died in the May."

"Oh," said Mrs. Ellery, and then, because she was young and kind and had no idea what to do, she said nothing at all, which was exactly right.

They sat for a while. The lounge was warm and the windows were streaming with condensation and beyond them the sea was going by in that flat interminable way it had gone by all day.

"Do you mind the cold?" said Mrs. Ellery presently.

"I mind it."

"It's the ice, they say. The steward says there's ice and the captain's gone south to miss it and we'll be in New York on Wednesday morning." She shifted the toddler, who had gone to sleep against her side with her mouth open. "I don't mind telling you I'll be glad to be off this ship. Not that it isn't beautiful. It is beautiful. But I've not slept properly since Southampton and I couldn't tell you why."

Arwen looked at her.

"Nor have I," she said.

"It's silly."

"It's not silly," said Arwen. "My mother used to say that a house knows when there's going to be a death in it. She'd say it and we'd laugh at her and then somebody would die." She handed the baby back. "Here. Take him. And take my advice, Mrs. Ellery, and don't laugh at me for it. When you go up on that deck on Wednesday and they tell you the ship's alongside, have your children in your arms before you get to the top of the stairs. Not your bags. Not your hat. Your children."

Mrs. Ellery looked at her with her head slightly on one side, the way a young woman looks at an older one who has said something strange.

"All right," she said.

"Promise me."

"All right," said Mrs. Ellery. "I promise. But we'll be in New York on Wednesday and it'll all seem very silly then, and I shall tease you about it at the pier."

"Do," said Arwen. "Tease me at the pier."

At half past two Arwen went up to the boat deck and Harold Lowe was not there, and a quartermaster told her that the fifth officer had been on watch since six and had gone below to get his dinner and his sleep and would be on the bridge again at six.

"Thank you," she said. "I'm so sorry to trouble you."

"No trouble, madam. Are you looking for somebody in particular?"

"No," said Arwen. "No. Not at all."

She walked the boat deck alone for twenty minutes in the bitter cold. There were men working at the boats — she saw that, and did not understand it, and remembered it later. Two of them were unlacing the canvas covers from the boats amidships and one of them was saying something to the other about the cold making the rope stiff, and then they stopped and did something else.

She stopped by one of the boats. Number 6, it said, painted on a small plaque. It was white and it hung in its davits and it was, she thought, much smaller than she had expected. She had imagined lifeboats the size of a launch. This was the size of a large rowing boat with pretensions.

*How many people,* she thought, *would go in that? Forty? Fifty?*

She counted the boats. Sixteen. She had counted them before, on Friday, and had not done anything with the number, and now she did: sixteen boats, and four of the flat collapsible things on the roof of the officers' quarters, and she did not know how many people they held, and she was not a woman who was good at sums, and it did not matter anyway, because it was Sunday afternoon and the sea was like a floor and the ship was the largest and finest and safest ship in the world, and there was a man below decks somewhere asleep with a slate heart under his vest.

She went down to her cabin and slept for an hour and dreamed of nothing at all.

---

The sunset began at about a quarter past six and it was, without any exaggeration, the most beautiful thing Arwen had ever seen, and she spent the rest of her life wishing she had not seen it.

It started in the west with a band of gold that widened and deepened until the whole sky from the horizon to the zenith was a colour for which she had no name — a red that was almost brown at the edges, like the inside of a furnace door, and through it the clouds went like rags. Then the sun went down into the sea, and the sea, which was flat and glassy, took the whole thing and doubled it, so that for about four minutes the ship appeared to be sailing through fire, and the ice on the foredeck rails and the brass and the white paint of the boats all turned the colour of blood.

People came out on deck to look at it. That was what she remembered most: that a hundred people came out on deck in the cold to look at it, and stood in a line at the rail, and said things like *would you look at that* and *I've never seen the like* and *it's like the end of the world*, and laughed, and went back inside.

Mrs. Thorne, beside her, said, "There's a saying. Red sky at night, sailor's delight."

"My father used to say the other one."

"What's the other one?"

"Red sky at morning, sailor take warning."

"That's the same saying."

"It's the other half of it," said Arwen. "My father only ever said the half that suited him."

She went down and dressed for dinner.

---

It was the last dinner, though nobody knew it, and it was the best dinner Arwen had eaten in years.

She wore the black and the pearls, and Nesta did her hair, and there was consommé and salmon and a saddle of lamb and a thing called *poulet à la Maryland* that Mrs. Thorne said was a crime against the bird, and there was champagne because Mr. Deveaux insisted, and there was a toast to the ship and a toast to the captain and a toast to the King, and afterwards, in the lounge, there was coffee and there were four men playing bridge and there was a woman at the piano playing something by Chopin with great feeling and the wrong tempo.

At half past nine Arwen went out on the promenade deck with her coat over her evening dress, and the cold hit her like a wall.

The stars were out. That was the thing. There were so many stars that the sky looked overcrowded, and the sea was black and flat and there was no wind at all, and the ship's lights laid two long yellow paths on the water and the water did not so much as wrinkle them.

She stood at the rail and looked at it for a while, and thought: *I am going to say it in half an hour, and then everything will be different, and I do not know whether it will be better.*

At ten o'clock she went up to the boat deck. The watch was changing; she could hear voices forward, and the door of the officers' quarters opened and shut, and a man came out shrugging on his coat and went forward at a run.

Harold Lowe came out five minutes later, and he was tired. She saw it at once, even in the dark — the particular heaviness in a man who has been four hours on a bridge in the cold with his eyes open — and he stopped when he saw her, and looked around, and there was nobody.

"You shouldn't be up here," he said.

"I know."

"It's half past nine at night and you're in evening dress and it's twenty-eight degrees."

"Twenty-nine," she said. "I looked."

He almost laughed. Then he rubbed his face with both hands. "Arwen. I'm no good tonight. I've been on the bridge since six and I've had a bad watch and I'm for my bed. Whatever it is—"

"It's nothing. It'll keep."

"It'll keep," he agreed, and then, because he was not a stupid man, he looked at her more carefully. "You've something to say to me."

"Yes."

"Say it."

"Not like this. Not at ten o'clock at night on a deck with a quartermaster twenty feet away." She heard her own voice, and it was not the voice she had rehearsed in. It was small and hurried and a little frightened. "Tomorrow. Tomorrow morning at six. I'll say it then, and I'll say it plain, and you'll not have to ask me twice."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he nodded at a man who had given him a course.

"Six o'clock," he said. "I'll have the coffee."

"Harold."

"Go to bed," he said. "You're blue with the cold. Go on."

She went. At the top of the aft staircase she turned and looked back, and he was still standing there in the dark with his hands in his pockets, watching her go, and then he turned and went forward, and that was the last time she saw him in the light.

---

She did not go to bed. She sat in the lounge until half past eleven with Mrs. Thorne and lost two shillings at bridge and thought about the morning.

"Bed," said Mrs. Thorne at last. "You've a face like a lamp."

"I'm going."

"Six o'clock?"

"Six o'clock."

"Then say it," said Mrs. Thorne, "and don't be clever about it. Cleverness is the death of everything."

In her cabin, Nesta had turned down the bed and left the small light on and a glass of water and a note that said, in her careful chapel-school hand: *Ma'am — your grey is pressed for the morning. The pheasant is not. N.*

Arwen laughed out loud, alone, at half past eleven at night, on the fourteenth of April, 1912.

Then she put out the light and lay down in the dark with her hands folded on her chest like a girl in a coffin, and thought about how she would say it — *Harold, I want you, I've always wanted you, and I'll go to New York and I'll tell Cedric the truth, and then I'm coming back, and if you'll have me I'll wait for you however long it takes, and if you go to the West Indies I'll write to you every week and I'll not let anybody burn them* — and the ship went on through the flat black water at twenty-two and a half knots, and the temperature fell to thirty-one degrees, and somewhere ahead of them in the dark a thing that had been floating south for two years came into the lane.

At twenty minutes to twelve, Arwen Vaughan was nearly asleep.

At twenty minutes to twelve, the lookout in the crow's nest rang three bells and picked up the telephone.

At twenty minutes to twelve, on the bridge, First Officer Murdoch said, "Hard a-starboard," and then, "Full astern."

At eleven forty, the ship hit the ice.

It was not a crash. That was the thing that everybody said afterwards, in every language, in every newspaper, for the rest of their lives: it was not a crash. It was a shudder, a grinding, a long soft tearing somewhere below and forward, like a fingernail drawn across the bottom of the world. The bed moved under her. The glass of water on the shelf rang once against the wall and was quiet.

Arwen sat up in the dark.

And her first thought — she was ashamed of it for years afterwards, and then she stopped being ashamed of it, because she came to understand that it was not shameful at all, that it was the most human thought she had ever had — her first thought was not *we are sinking*, and it was not *God help us*, and it was not even *the boats*.

Her first thought was: *I have not said it yet.*

---

# VI

## A Slight Shock

### 14 April, 11.40 p.m. – 15 April, 1.45 a.m.

For a little while, nothing happened.

That was what nobody ever believed afterwards, and Arwen stopped trying to explain it. There was no alarm. There was no shouting. The engines, which had been a presence under everything for four days, a pulse you could feel in your teeth, wound down and stopped, and the ship went on through the water at eleven knots and then nine and then five, and the water along the hull made a sound that she had never heard before because the engines had always covered it — a soft, steady, hissing wash, like rain on a roof.

She got up. She put on her dressing gown and her slippers and opened the door of her cabin and looked into the corridor.

There was a man at the far end in a nightshirt and a smoking jacket, holding a candle, which was absurd, because the electric lights were on.

"Did you feel it?" he said.

"I felt something."

"Machinery," he said. "Must be. Bound to be, on a ship this size. Bound to be a bit of machinery now and then."

"Yes," said Arwen.

"Do you think we've stopped?"

"I think we've stopped."

"Well," said the man, and looked at his candle, and blew it out, and went back into his cabin and shut the door.

She waited. She stood in her own doorway with her hand on the frame and waited, and nothing happened, and the hissing went on, and somewhere far above there was a noise of men running — not many, not a crowd, just three or four pairs of boots going somewhere in a hurry.

Nesta came out of the outer cabin with her hair in a plait and her eyes wide. "Ma'am?"

"It's all right."

"What's happened?"

"I don't know."

"Should we—"

"Get dressed," said Arwen. "Both of us. Warmly. Properly. And put your boots on, not your shoes."

That was the first sensible thing she did, and she never knew where it came from. It came from her mother, probably, who had been through a colliery disaster in '85 and had never afterwards been able to hear a fire alarm without putting her coat on.

They dressed. Arwen put on the grey with the braid and two petticoats and the heavy coat and the boots with the laces, and she took her jewel case out of the drawer and looked at it, and then put it back, and then took it out again and put it in her coat pocket, and then took it out a third time and left it on the bed, where it was found, four days later, by a steward of the *Mackay-Bennett*, in a cabin on B deck that had flooded to the ceiling.

Nesta was in her coat with her hat in her hand and her face white as paper.

"Where's your other boot?"

"I've only the one on."

"Then find the other boot."

"Ma'am—"

"Nesta. Find the other boot."

Nesta found the other boot. They went up.

---

The corridor on B deck was full of people in various stages of undress, and every single one of them was calm, and the calm was the most frightening thing Arwen had ever seen.

A steward was going along with a tray of something. A woman in a fur coat over a nightgown was laughing at something her husband had said. Two men in evening dress, who had clearly come straight from the smoking room, were standing with their hands in their pockets discussing the matter in the tone of men discussing a delayed train.

"I tell you it's the ice. We've struck ice."

"Well, if we've struck ice, we've struck ice. She's not going to sink, is she?"

"She's not going to sink," said the other man. "She can't sink. She's got sixteen compartments."

"Sixteen?"

"Sixteen. And if any four of them flood she'll still float. It's in the *Shipbuilder*. My brother-in-law's in the trade."

Arwen went up the grand staircase, and at the top of it she met the steward Penney, who was carrying a great armful of lifebelts and was sweating.

"Penney."

"Madam. You want to go up on the boat deck, madam, and put your belt on—"

"What's happened?"

"I don't know, madam, only we've been told to get the belts out and get the passengers up top, and Mr. McElroy says there's no danger but it's best to be on the safe side." He shifted the armful. "Here — take a belt, madam. Take two."

"Penney, is it serious?"

The boy looked at her. He was twenty-two years old and had a Sunday suit at home in Southampton and a mother and a girl he was going to marry in the autumn, and he looked at her for a moment with the sweat running down his temple and said, "I don't know, madam. I've never been in a ship that stopped."

---

The boat deck at twenty past twelve was the busiest place she had ever seen and the least organized, and nobody was running.

That was the thing that took her a while to understand: that the noise was not panic, because there was no panic yet. It was work. Men were working, and the work had the quality of work being done against time by people who have not been told how much time there is.

The canvas covers were off the boats. Men were lifting the oars out and laying them along the deck and doing something with the falls, and the falls were stiff with cold and would not run, and there was a great deal of swearing, and it was not frightened swearing; it was the ordinary swearing of men doing a hard job in the dark.

The steam was the worst of it. Somewhere up forward, the boilers had been vented through the funnels, and the noise was like the end of the world — a roar so loud that a man standing a foot away from you had to shout into your ear and even then you only got half of it. It went on and on. It stopped at about twenty to one, and when it stopped, the silence was worse.

Arwen stood at the top of the stairs with Nesta's hand in hers and looked for Harold Lowe and could not find him.

She saw the captain. He was standing by the wheelhouse with his hands behind his back, talking to a man with a notebook, and even at that distance she could see that his face had gone the colour of clay. She saw a stout man in a dinner jacket — she learned afterwards it was Mr. Ismay — walking up and down by the boats with his hands in his pockets, and twice she saw him speak to an officer and point, and the officer would go and do something.

She saw Mr. Murdoch on the starboard side, and Mr. Lightoller on the port side, and Mr. Moody with a lantern, and a man she did not know at all who turned out to be Mr. Boxhall, and none of them was Harold.

"Stay by me," she said to Nesta, "and hold on to the rail and do not let go of it for anything."

There was a crowd at the rail — a hundred people, two hundred, all in coats and furs and lifebelts, and the lifebelts made them look like a gathering of enormous strange birds. Somebody was laughing. A woman behind Arwen was saying, over and over, in a bright social voice, "Well, I do think they might have told us, I do think they might have told us," and her husband was saying, "They have told us, my dear, they've told us to come up here, and we've come up here, and now we wait."

At the head of the stairs, an officer she did not know put his hand up.

"Women and children to the port side," he said. "Ladies, this way, if you please. Boats are being prepared. Women and children to the port side."

Arwen did not move.

"Ma'am," said the officer, "if you'd go to the port side—"

"I'm waiting for someone."

"There's no waiting, ma'am, we're getting the boats away—"

"Then I'll wait here," she said, "and I'll go when I've found him," and the officer looked at her for a moment and then said something under his breath and went on to the next person.

At about a quarter past twelve, the band came out.

They came up the aft staircase in their white coats with their instruments, eight of them, and they stood on the boat deck between the second and third funnels where there was room, and they played. It was not a hymn. It was a ragtime tune, one Arwen had heard in the lounge the night before, and it was being played rather well and rather quietly and entirely without irony, and the effect on the crowd was extraordinary: three hundred people who had been standing in silence at the rail turned round and began, some of them, to smile.

A woman beside Arwen said, "Oh, good. They've got the band up."

"Yes," said Arwen.

"That's a good sign, isn't it? If they've got the band up." She was a small woman in a sealskin coat with her hair done for dinner and no hat. "It stands to reason. They wouldn't have the band up if it was going to be—"

"No," said Arwen. "They wouldn't."

The woman nodded, satisfied, and went on listening to the music with her head slightly on one side, and Arwen stood beside her and listened to the music too, and neither of them said anything else, and the band played on while the water came up the forward deck and the rockets went up out of the dark behind them.

---

The first rocket went up at about a quarter to one.

It went up from the starboard side with a *thump* and a long hiss and then burst overhead in a shower of white stars, and the whole deck turned its face up to look at it, and a woman said, "Ohh," the way people say it at fireworks, and somebody else applauded, two or three claps, and then stopped.

The second one went up four minutes later.

By the third one, nobody was applauding.

---

She found him at twenty past midnight, or he found her.

She was at the port rail by then, because an officer had been firm with her, and she had gone because Nesta was shivering and because standing in one place with a frozen girl was worse than moving. The boats on the port side were being swung out and the falls were being overhauled, and men were standing in the bottom of the boats with lanterns, and the davits creaked like the joints of something enormous.

And then a voice behind her, hoarse from shouting, said: "Arwen."

She turned.

He had no cap and no greatcoat. He had a whistle on a lanyard round his neck and a revolver in a holster on his belt, and there was a smear of grease down the front of his white jacket, and he looked at her with an expression she had never seen on him and never saw again — not fear; something more like fury, turned inward.

"What are you doing up here?"

"I came up when the ship stopped."

"Why aren't you in a boat?"

"There weren't any boats."

"There are boats now." He took her by the arm — not gently, and she did not mind — and turned her round. "Listen to me. Listen. This is not a drill and it's not a bit of machinery and I've not got time to be polite about it. The ship is going to sink. Do you understand what I'm saying to you? She's going to sink, and it's going to be inside the hour, and there'll be a deal of people in the water, and if you're one of them you'll be dead in ten minutes, because it's twenty-eight degrees. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Then get in a boat."

"Where's Nesta to go?"

"Where's—" He looked at Nesta, who was standing with her hat in her hand and her mouth open. "She goes in a boat too. Both of you. Now."

"Harold."

"Now, Arwen."

"Harold, listen to me." She got her hand free and put it flat on his chest, on the grease, over the place where the slate heart was, and she felt him stop, the way a horse stops. "I was going to say it at six o'clock. I'm saying it now. I want you. I've always wanted you. I'll not marry Cedric and I'll not go back to the house with the lift in it and I'll wait for you however long it takes, and I don't care if it's the West Indies, I'll write to you every week and—"

"Arwen."

"— and I'll not let anybody burn them."

He stood absolutely still. Behind him, a man was shouting for the falls to be let go, and a rocket went up and lit the whole deck white, and the light went across his face and he had his eyes shut.

"Say it again," he said.

"I want you."

"Right," said Harold Lowe. He opened his eyes. "Right. Now get in the boat."

"Harold—"

"Arwen, I've forty people to put in a boat and if I stand here another minute I'll not be fit to do it, and if I do it badly somebody'll die. So I'm going to say this once and then I'm going to go and be an officer." He took her face in both his hands, and his hands were freezing and shaking, and he kissed her on the mouth in front of two hundred people on the boat deck of the *Titanic*, and then he let her go. "In the boat. Both of you. I'll find you after."

"Promise me."

"I promise you," he said. "Now *go*."

---

The first boat on the port side went at about a quarter to one, and it went with twenty-eight people in it, and it was built to hold sixty-five.

Arwen did not see it. She was being moved along the deck by a steward with a lantern, and Nesta was holding the back of her coat, and there was a queue, or the beginning of a queue, a loose straggling line of women in furs and women in shawls and children, and at the head of it Mr. Lightoller was standing on the rail with his hand out, saying, over and over, in the same level voice:

"Women and children. This way. Step lively, please. Women and children."

A woman in front of Arwen said, "I won't go without my husband."

"Where's your husband, madam?"

"He's gone to get the luggage."

Mr. Lightoller said something to her that Arwen could not hear, and the woman began to cry, and then she got into the boat.

The boat was swinging out over the water now, thirty feet above it, and the sea down there was black and flat and the boat hung in the air with the men in it holding the lanterns up, and there was nothing under it. Arwen looked over the rail and thought, with a clarity that surprised her: *that is thirty feet. That is a house. If that rope slips, those people die.*

"Madam." A steward's hand at her elbow. "This way, madam."

"Wait."

"There's no waiting, madam, there's a boat going."

"Then let it go," said Arwen. "There are women behind me."

She stepped back out of the line and put her hand on Nesta's shoulder and pushed her forward.

"Go on."

"Ma'am?"

"Get in the boat."

"Not without you."

"Nesta." Arwen took her by both arms. "You're nineteen years old and you can't swim and I can and I'm not going to stand here arguing with a girl from Blaenau while the ship goes down. Get in the boat."

"Come with me!"

"I'll come in the next one."

"Ma'am—"

"There's a good girl," said Arwen, and kissed her on the forehead, and gave her a push.

Nesta went over the rail into the boat and turned round and held out her hand, and Arwen did not take it, and the boat went down the ship's side with the falls creaking and the women crying and one man's voice saying "Easy — *easy* — hold her —" and Nesta's face getting smaller and smaller, white against the black water, with her mouth open, shouting something that Arwen could not hear over the steam and the shouting and the sea.

Twenty-eight people went in that boat. There were spaces for thirty-seven more.

At the rail beside Arwen, a woman in a black coat with diamonds in her ears said, "They're not even half full. Why aren't they filling them?"

"Because there's nobody to fill them with," said Arwen. "Everybody's standing here saying it can't sink."

---

By one o'clock the ship had begun to go down by the head, and it was not a thing you could argue with any more.

You could see it in the waterline. You could see it along the deck, which had begun to slope, so that the boats forward were lower over the sea than the boats aft, and men were having to be careful how they walked. You could see it in the fact that the well deck forward, where the third-class passengers had been kept back behind gates for an hour and a half, was now under water, and that the people who had been behind those gates were coming up the decks in a river, hundreds of them, with their bundles and their children and their faces.

Arwen stood at the rail by the second boat and watched them come, and something in her chest turned over and would not turn back.

They were so *quiet*. That was the thing she never got over. She had expected — she did not know what she had expected. She had expected panic, and instead there was a great slow quiet river of people coming up out of the dark with their babies on their shoulders, and the ones at the front had begun to understand what was happening, and the ones at the back had not, and the ones in the middle were being told by the ones in front.

A woman came past her carrying a suitcase and a child and a birdcage with no bird in it, and Arwen thought, with a lurch that was almost nausea: *Queenstown. The boy with the cage.*

"Madam." It was Mr. Lightoller, and his face was grey with cold and effort. "You've been standing here an hour. Get in the boat."

"There are women coming up from below."

"There are women here now, and the boat is going, and I'll not stand arguing—"

"Then don't argue," said Arwen. "Put them in. I'll wait for the next one."

Lightoller looked at her for a moment. Something crossed his face — not admiration, she thought; irritation, and something under it.

"Then get out of the way of the rail," he said, "and stay out of it," and turned away.

That was the moment, she decided later, when it became certain. Not the water, not the rockets, not the guns. It was an officer telling her to get out of the way: it was the exact moment at which she stopped being a passenger and became part of the ship.

---

Up forward, a man was shouting himself hoarse about the collapsibles.

Arwen went forward along the sloping deck, holding on to the rail, past the second boat and the third, and the crowd here had thinned and the ship's crew had thickened, and there were men on the roof of the officers' quarters with ropes, hauling at something.

He was there. She saw him before he saw her: standing on top of the officers' quarters with his jacket off and his shirt sleeves rolled, hauling on a rope with three other men, and the collapsible boat was coming up over the edge of the roof inch by inch on a pair of oars lashed together as a slide, and he was shouting at them to *heave*, and they were heaving, and somebody slipped and the whole thing went back a foot, and Harold Lowe said a word in Welsh that made even Arwen blink.

Then they got it over, and it dropped onto the deck with a boom, and he stood up and wiped his forehead with his forearm and looked down the deck and saw her.

He came down off the roof in one movement, swinging off the edge, and landed badly, and did not care.

"What are you still doing here?"

"There's no room in the boats."

"There's room in the boats. There's room in every blessed boat on this ship, there's room for four hundred more people and they're going away half empty because nobody'll believe—" He stopped himself. "Where's the girl?"

"In a boat."

"Right. Good." He looked at her, and his jaw worked. "Go aft. There's boats aft. There's a boat going from the after end, the collapsible, they're getting it off the roof—"

"Harold, the water's coming up the deck."

"Then go up the boat deck and stay there and when a boat goes, get in it, and don't ask anybody's permission." He took her by the shoulders. "Arwen. I'm going to be in a boat. I've been told off to the port side and I'll be in one of them before this is over, and when I am, I'll come back. Do you hear me? I'll come back for the ones in the water. And if you're in the water, I'll find you."

"Harold—"

"I've never broken a promise to you," he said. "I'm not starting tonight."

Then a man was shouting his name from forward, and he let go of her, and ran.

---

At twenty past one the water reached the forward well deck and began to come up the stairs.

At half past one the *Titanic* was down by the head with the sea over her forecastle, and the band had stopped playing in the lounge and had come out onto the boat deck and was playing a ragtime tune that nobody could hear.

At twenty-five to two, Arwen Vaughan, aged twenty-nine years and one month, stood at the after end of the boat deck of the *Titanic*, holding on to a rail that was now tilted at an angle she could feel in her knees, and watched a white boat go down the ship's side into the dark, and in the bottom of it, standing with his hand on the tiller and his cap gone and his hair in his eyes, was Harold Lowe.

He looked up. He was forty feet away and going down, and she saw him look up, and she raised her hand.

He did not wave. He put his hand flat on his chest, over the heart, and held it there — and then the boat reached the water with a slap, and the falls were let go, and the oars came out, and Boat 14 pulled away from the side of the *Titanic* into the black water at twenty-five minutes to two in the morning.

Arwen watched it go. Then she turned round and looked aft along the deck, at the sloping ship, at the people, at the boats that were left.

There was one. A collapsible, up on the roof of the officers' quarters, and men were trying to get it down, and it was too heavy, and the ship was going down by the head, and the water was coming up the deck behind them like a hand.

---

# VII

## Boat Fourteen

### 15 April, 1.10 a.m. – 2.20 a.m.

**The port side, 1.10 a.m.**

There were too many people at Boat 14 and not enough of them were women.

That was the arithmetic Harold Lowe had been doing for forty minutes and it was making him savage. The boat hung at the rail with its bow slung out over the water, and his men had the falls and the oars in and the plug in and a lantern in the stern, and there were perhaps sixty people pressing at the rail behind it and less than half of them were women, and the boat would take sixty-five and there was not one single soul in that crowd who believed the ship was going down.

"Women and children," he said. "Ladies. This way. Come on, now, come on."

A woman came. Then another. Then a steward brought four more up from the aft deck, shawled women with their hair down and one of them carrying a baby wrapped in a blanket that had somebody's initials on it, and Lowe put them in himself, one at a time, with a hand under the elbow, and said, "That's it. Sit down. Sit *down* in the boat, madam, don't stand up in it, there's a good woman."

Behind them the crowd was thickening.

"Mr. Lowe—" It was one of the able seamen, a man called Scarrott. "Sir, they're coming over the rail from the aft—"

"Then hold them."

"There's too many."

"Then hold them anyway."

The ship was down by the head now. He could feel it through the soles of his boots and he could see it in the waterline: the forward well deck gone, the sea reaching up the stairs in a slow climb like something with a plan. The boats forward were already in the water; someone had got Boat 4 away, or half away; there had been a mess at Boat 2 and a worse mess at Boat 6 with the women refusing to go and a quartermaster who would not pull.

And there were still — he did the sum again, and got the same answer, and it was a filthy answer — there were still more than fifteen hundred people on that ship and there were four boats left.

"Sir." Scarrott again, and this time his voice had gone up. "Sir—"

Lowe turned round.

The crowd had come off the rail and onto the deck in a body, thirty or forty men, and they were not rushing the boat exactly; they were moving toward it the way water moves toward a low place, and the men at the front had their hands out and their eyes on the gunwale, and one of them was already climbing.

Lowe drew his revolver and fired three shots into the air over the side of the ship.

The noise was astonishing. It went flat across the water and came back off nothing, and the crowd stopped dead like a horse at a wall, and a woman screamed, and in the moment afterwards there was no sound on that deck at all but the sea.

"Get back," said Harold Lowe.

Nobody moved.

"Get back from that rail. The next man that puts a leg over it I'll shoot him like a dog, and don't think I won't, because I've had a bellyful tonight and I'll not lose forty women to save one coward." His voice did not shake. That was the thing he was proudest of, afterwards, and also the thing he was least proud of, and he never entirely resolved it. "Women and children. That's the rule. It's not my rule, it's the rule of the sea, and I've seen men die for it and I'll see it kept tonight if I have to keep it myself."

A man at the back said something about his mother.

"Then your mother can get in the boat," said Lowe, "and you can stand there and be a man about it."

Somebody laughed. It was a horrible laugh, high and cracked, but it broke the thing, and the crowd came apart, and the women came forward again, and Lowe put eleven more of them into Boat 14 in about four minutes with the sweat running into his eyes.

"Right," he said. "Lower away. Smartly now, both falls together, don't let her cock up — *together*, mind."

And the boat went down the ship's side, and he was standing in the stern of it with his hand on the tiller, and he looked up once.

She was forty feet above him at the after end of the boat deck. She had one hand on the rail. She raised it.

He put his hand flat on his chest, where the slate heart was, and held it there.

Then the boat slapped the water and he was shouting at the men to cast off the falls and get the oars out, because there was a ship above them that was going to come down, and the only question was how far away they could get before it did.

---

**The boat deck, 1.50 a.m.**

Arwen did not watch Boat 14 row away. She turned her back on it deliberately, the way you turn your back on a fire to go and do something in the next room, and she walked forward along a deck that was now tilted at an angle her body had begun to compensate for without asking her.

There were four boats left and three of them were on the other side.

She went forward.

Halfway along the deck a steward with a lantern stopped her and said, "Madam, they're going from the starboard side, there's a boat going at the forward starboard and Mr. Murdoch's got her, go round — round the front, madam, not through the officers' quarters, they'll not have you through there—"

So she went round the front of the officers' quarters, along the narrow strip of deck where the wind came over the top of the wheelhouse and took her breath, and came out on the starboard side.

The deck was wet with something she did not look at closely. She passed a man sitting on a hatch cover with his head in his hands and no lifebelt, and she stopped and said, "There are belts in the chart room—" and he said, without looking up, "Go away."

The forward end of the boat deck was a different country. The bridge was there, and the wheelhouse, and above the wheelhouse the officers' quarters, and on the roof of the officers' quarters there was still one collapsible boat and forty men trying to get it down.

The other one had gone. It had gone over on the port side half an hour before, and there had been some trouble with it, and it was somewhere out there in the dark now, and there were men on it.

The one on the roof was called A. She learned that afterwards; at the time it was simply a great flat canvas-sided thing on a roof, and the men were trying to slide it down onto a pair of oars lashed together, and the oars were bending, and somebody at the back was shouting to the men at the front to *hold it, hold it, hold it*, and a man was up on the roof with a lantern and his cap gone, and it was Mr. Moody, and he was nineteen years old and he was crying and working at the same time, which Arwen thought afterwards was the bravest thing she saw all night.

"Sir — sir, the oars are going—"

"Then get more oars. Get anything. Get the—" That was Mr. Murdoch, and his voice had gone to pieces, and he was standing at the foot of the roof with his hands up as if he were holding the whole thing by main force. "Get it *down*, for God's sake, get it down—"

A woman came past Arwen, alone, with no coat and no belt, walking aft, and said, in a conversational voice, "Have they gone?"

"The boats? Yes."

"All of them?"

"Not all."

"Well," said the woman, and went on walking aft, and Arwen never saw her again and did not know her name.

The collapsible came down off the roof in one long tearing rush and hit the deck with a boom that she felt in her teeth, and then there was a confusion of oars and shouting, and Mr. Murdoch was saying, "To the rail — get it to the rail — take an oar, take an oar—"

Arwen put her hands on the gunwale of the collapsible and pushed.

It was like pushing a piano. It weighed nothing and everything; it moved in lurches and stopped, and the deck was tilting, and the men around her were gasping and swearing and she had her shoulder against the canvas side and her boots slipping and her hands going numb, and someone was saying, "Once more — *once* more—"

Then she heard a woman's voice behind her, twenty yards aft, saying, "Please. Please. Is this one going?"

Arwen turned round.

There was a boat at the forward starboard davits. It was one of the last two boats on that side of the ship and it was already in the falls and it was at the rail with people going into it, and Mr. Murdoch had turned away from the roof and was there, and Arwen went after him without deciding to.

Mary Ellery was standing at the rail with the baby against her shoulder and the little girl by the hand.

She had done everything she had been told. She had come up onto the boat deck at half past twelve with both children in her arms, just as Arwen had told her on Sunday afternoon, and it had been of no use at all: she had been sent aft and then forward and then aft again, and she had stood in four queues, and every boat she had reached had been going from the other side of the ship, or had gone already, or had never been a boat at all but a crowd of people standing around one.

"Mrs. Ellery."

The woman looked at her without any recognition whatever — she had gone somewhere behind her own eyes and shut the door — and said, "Nobody'll take us. I've been to four of them."

"Women and children," said Murdoch. "This way. Madam — in the boat, if you please."

"There's no room," said a man in the boat. "There's no room, sir, she's full—"

"There's room," said Murdoch. "There's room in her for twenty more and I'll not hear another word about it. Madam. *Now*, if you please."

And Arwen looked at the boat, and the boat had room in it, and she was so tired.

That was the thing she had not expected: that at ten past two in the morning on a sinking ship she would be so tired that the offer of a seat felt like an offer of sleep. Her hands came off the gunwale of the collapsible behind her. She took a step toward the rail.

Then she saw the woman's face.

It did not plead. Arwen thought about that for years, and it was the thing she found hardest to explain and the thing she never got over: that in the last two minutes of the *Titanic*, a woman with two children and no coat looked at a strange woman on a boat deck and did not plead. She simply registered, quite calmly, that there were two of them and one place.

Arwen stopped.

"Take her," she said.

"Madam—"

"Take her. She's got two children and I've got none."

"Get *in* the boat," said Murdoch.

"No," said Arwen. She stepped back, and her voice came out perfectly steady and she was never able to explain it. "Mr. Murdoch, I'm going to go and stand over there by the second funnel and I'm going to hold on to the rail, and if you shout at me again I shall scream, and that would be a great pity, because there are ladies present."

Murdoch looked at her for one second. He had a wife and children in England and eleven minutes to live and he did not know either of those things.

Then he said, "God help you," and turned away and said, "Next. Is there anybody else? *Is there anybody else?*"

Mary Ellery got in over the rail with the baby and the little girl, and somebody took the baby from her, and she turned round and looked up at Arwen and said, "You told me. On Sunday. You told me to have them in my arms."

"I know."

"How did you know?"

"I didn't," said Arwen. "Sit down in the boat and hold on to them and don't stand up."

"Your name—"

"Vaughan. Arwen Vaughan. And when your husband meets you at the pier, Mrs. Ellery, you'll tell him that a Welshwoman with a pheasant in her hat gave you a piece of advice on a Sunday afternoon and that she was right."

Then the boat went down the ship's side, and Arwen watched it until it was on the water, and then she went back along the deck and put her hands on the gunwale of the collapsible again and pushed.

They got it to the rail.

They got it over the rail, and it went down into the sea the wrong way up and came back the right way up, and there was a terrible minute when it was half in and half out and men were shouting, and then it was afloat and there was nobody in it and the water was coming over the deck.

---

**The water**

It came in over the forward end of the boat deck at about ten past two, and it came in a way that no one had imagined.

They had all imagined a wave. There was no wave. What happened was that the whole forward part of the ship simply went under, quietly, the way a stone goes under, and the sea came back along the deck in a shallow running flood about a foot deep and *it was warm*.

That was the thing that undid her. It was warm. After five hours of a cold that had gone through her coat and her boots and into the marrow of her, the water came over her ankles like a bath, and she thought — she actually thought, for about a second and a half — *oh. Oh, thank God.*

Then her legs went out from under her.

The deck was tilting the other way now. The ship was going down by the head and coming up at the stern, and the boat deck had become a slope of perhaps twenty degrees, and the water running along it was fast, and Arwen went down on her hands and knees in it and the cold took her breath with a physical blow.

She got hold of the rail. She got both hands on it, and she pulled herself up, and she stood in running water to her knees with the rail under her armpits, and the water went past her and over her and around her, and she held on.

All around her, people were letting go.

She could not see them. The lights were still on — that was the horror of it, the lights were still on and yellow and steady, and the ship was going down with all her lights burning, and the sea was black, and between the yellow and the black there was nothing to see. But she could hear them. She could hear the whole ship: a sound like a great crowd in a great hall, and the sound was made of every individual voice on that ship saying its own thing, and she would hear it for the rest of her life, and in the fifty years afterwards she never once described it to anybody except one person.

Something struck her in the back — a deck chair, a piece of wood, she never knew — and her hands came off the rail.

She went under.

It was not like going under in the sea at Barmouth, where the water was green and full of light and you could see the stones on the bottom. This was black and cold and utterly silent, and she had no idea which way was up, and she was in a coat and two petticoats and boots, and the weight of it was pulling her down and she was thinking, quite clearly, *this is how it happens, then.*

Then her head came out into the air.

She got one breath — one — and it was mostly water, and she was under again, and this time she thought about the swimming bath and the marble step and six inches of water, and she thought about her mother, and she thought, with a fury that was almost comic, *I have not said it yet.*

And then her hand hit something solid.

It was a rope. It was the falls of the collapsible, hanging down from the davit, and her fingers closed on it and slid, and closed again, and she got both hands on it and hung there in the black water with her face six inches above the surface and the water slapping at her mouth.

The ship was going. She could feel it in the rope: a long slow pull, the whole hull turning over on its way down, and the rope was going with it, and she was going to go with the rope.

"Here—" Somebody's hand closed on her collar. "Here. *Here.* Take her — take her hand —"

She was hauled up out of the water by the back of her coat, and somebody got an arm under hers, and she was in a boat — a flat, awash, canvas-sided thing, half full of water, with people standing in it up to their thighs and hanging on to the sides, and the water was coming over the gunwale in a steady pour and there was no floor under her feet at all, only water and the bodies of people.

She got her arm over the gunwale and hung on, and someone put a hand flat on the top of her head and pushed her down — "Keep your head down, ma'am, keep it *down*, she's going—" — and then the noise came.

The *Titanic* went under at twenty minutes past two in the morning, and she did not go quietly.

She went with a roar that Arwen felt in her bones and heard as a kind of enormous tearing, and the sea boiled, and the thing that had been the deck under her feet fell away, and the water all around them lifted and surged and threw the collapsible up and out and away, so that for one moment they were on the crest of a wave made by a hundred thousand tons of ship going down, and then they came down into the trough of it and the water closed over them and came up again.

Then it was quiet.

The lights went out — she did not see when; they were there and then they were not — and the stars were there instead, and the sky was so full of stars that it looked like Frost Fair glitter on a black cloth, and the sea was black and flat and there was no wind and no ship.

And then, from all around them, out of the dark, the screaming began.

---

**Boat 14, 2.25 a.m.**

Harold Lowe had his boat a quarter of a mile off and he had the oars out and he had his lantern up and he was counting.

"How many?"

"Forty-one, sir. Maybe forty-three."

"Forty-three," he said. "In a boat for sixty-five."

He said it flatly, and the women in the boat looked at him and said nothing.

He had spent the last hour doing the only thing he could think of, which was to make a fleet out of a shambles. He had found Boat 12 and Boat 10 and Boat 4 and Collapsible D, and he had shouted himself hoarse getting them to come together, and he had got them tied bow to bow and stern to stern with what rope there was, so that they would be a bigger thing on the water than five small things, and so that if a ship came they would be found. He had put a woman at the tiller of one of them because there was nobody else. He had shouted at a quartermaster in Boat 6 to come alongside and take people off, and the man had refused, and Harold Lowe had called him a name that he was later obliged to repeat in a Senate committee room in Washington in front of a great many newspapermen.

And now five boats were tied together in the dark, with about two hundred and fifty people in them, and the *Titanic* had gone down, and the screaming had started.

It was not a sound he had ever heard. He had heard men drown. He had heard a man scream once in a hold in the Plate when a hatch cover came down on him. This was not that. This was a thousand people in water at twenty-eight degrees, and they were not screaming words, and it went on and on and on.

"Sir," said a woman behind him, very quietly. "Sir."

He did not turn round.

"Sir. Are we going back?"

He stood in the stern with his hand on the tiller and his lantern in the other hand and he did not answer her for a long moment.

He was doing sums. He had been doing sums his whole life — tides, coal, distances, the run of a ship, the weight of a boat — and now he was doing the worst sum he had ever done, which was this: that a boat with forty-three people in it, if it went back into that water with a thousand people in it, would be swamped and everybody in it would die, and that if it waited too long there would be nobody left to save.

"Not yet," he said.

"Sir—"

"Not *yet*." His voice cracked. "You don't understand. If I take this boat into that I'll lose every soul in it. There's a thousand people in the water and they'll go for the boat and they'll swamp it and they'll drown each other getting in, and I'll have forty-three dead instead of forty-three saved. We wait till the edge is off it. We wait till they're — " He stopped.

Till they're quieter, he had been going to say. Till they've stopped.

He looked at the lantern in his hand. Then he passed it to the woman beside him without a word, and put both hands over his face for a count of five, and took them down again, and said:

"Right. Get me the boat's crew. Four men. And any man in this boat that's got arms in his coat and isn't afraid to use them."

"Sir?"

"I'm going back," said Harold Lowe. "I'm going back and I'll take whoever's alive, and I'll do it without a light so they don't see us coming, and if a man tries to climb in over the gunwale and won't be told, I'll hit him with an oar and I'll not lose a minute's sleep over it. Who's for going back?"

There was a silence in the boat.

Then a man said, "I'll go."

And another said, "Aye. All right."

"Right," said Lowe. He picked up an oar. "Unship that rope. We're casting off from the others and we're going back to the wreck, and I want every woman in this boat to sit down and hold on and say nothing, and if there's a man among you that's got a flask, now's the time to be a Christian about it."

Somebody laughed. It was the same cracked laugh he had heard on the boat deck an hour before, and it was the best sound he had heard all night.

Then they cut the rope and turned the boat's head round, and Harold Lowe, fifth officer of the *R.M.S. Titanic*, twenty-nine years old, with a revolver in his pocket and a slate heart on a bootlace round his neck, rowed back into the dark toward the sound of the screaming.

# VIII

## The Water

### 15 April, 2.20 a.m. – 3.40 a.m.

There were about thirty of them in the collapsible and there was room for sixty, and the reason there was room for sixty was that the canvas sides had never been raised.

Arwen understood this slowly, the way one understands a thing in a fever. She was standing — she thought she was standing; she was being held up by the press of bodies and by the gunwale under her arm — in a flat canvas tray in the middle of the North Atlantic, and the water inside the boat was the same water as the water outside the boat, except that the boat had a wooden bottom somewhere under her feet, six feet down, and the canvas walls were folded flat and would not keep out a bath.

Somebody had got the plug in. Somebody had made a grab at the sides. It had not been enough.

The water came up to her chest and it did not move, because the boat did not move, because the sea was like a floor. Every now and then the whole thing surged a little and the water went up to her armpits, and then it settled again.

Around them, the sea was full of people, and the sound they made was not a human sound.

It went on for perhaps twenty minutes. She did not have a watch — hers was in the cabin, with the jewel case, under four fathoms of water — and she had no way at all of measuring time except by the sound, which was thinning.

It thinned. That was the horror of it. It did not stop; it *thinned*, the way a crowd thins, and you could begin to hear individuals in it: a man calling a name over and over, a child — no, not a child; she made herself listen and it was a woman — a woman somewhere out to the left saying "Oh, oh, oh," steadily, like a person rocking.

And then, quite close, maybe thirty feet away, a man began to blow a whistle.

He blew it in bursts. Three long, three short. Then a pause. Then three long, three short.

"Somebody's got a whistle," said a man in the boat.

"Then shout to him," said Arwen.

"It's no good. He can't swim to us and we can't get to him, the sides are down—"

"Then shout to him anyway," she said, "so he knows there's somebody," and the man shouted, "Hullo! *Hullo!* Hold on, we've got a boat!" and the whistle stopped for a moment, and then started again, three long, three short, and went on for a very long time, and then it stopped, and did not start again.

Later — it was years later, reading a book — she found out that there had been a whistle on the water that night, that more than one boat had heard it, that it had been reported and described and argued over, and that nobody ever established whose it was. She read the passage twice and then put the book down and went out into the garden and stood there for a while.

---

There was a woman beside her, on her left, whose name she never learned.

She was young — twenty-two or twenty-three — and she had a wedding ring and no coat and her hair had come down and she had been in the water as long as Arwen and she had stopped shivering, which Arwen knew, from somewhere, from a book or from a doctor's waiting room, was the worst thing that could happen.

"Talk to me," said Arwen.

"I'm all right."

"Talk to me anyway. What's your name?"

"Bridget."

"Bridget what?"

"Bridget — " The woman's teeth were going so hard she could not get the next word out. "Bridget Nolan. I'm going to — I'm going to Chicago. My sister's in Chicago."

"Tell me about your sister."

"She's got a — she's got a shop. A bakery. She says there's a room for me and I'm to work in the shop and we'll — we'll get a girl to do the bread, and I'll do the counter, and there's a young man—" Her voice went away.

"Bridget."

"There's a young man in Chicago," said Bridget Nolan, "and he doesn't know it yet, but he's going to marry me."

"Does he have a name?"

"He's called Michael and he's got a bicycle."

"Then you'll have a bicycle too," said Arwen, "and you'll ride it in the park on Sundays with your sister's bread in the basket, and when you're sixty you'll be very stout and very happy and you'll tell your grandchildren about the night you were in a boat in the middle of the ocean and a Welshwoman wouldn't let you go to sleep."

Bridget Nolan laughed. It took her a long time to do it and it was not much of a laugh, but she did it, and then she said, "Say something else in Welsh."

"*Rwy'n dy garu di,*" said Arwen.

"What's that mean?"

"It means I meant it."

And Bridget Nolan said, "Oh," and put her head down on Arwen's shoulder, and Arwen held her up with one arm and the gunwale with the other, and after a while she realized that the woman had gone to sleep anyway, and that she was still breathing, and that there was nothing at all that could be done about either.

---

The boat was a republic of about thirty people and it had no government.

There was a fireman in it named — she thought — Doran, who had come up out of the water black to the elbows and who had taken charge in the way that some men do, by doing things, and he had got the oars out of the bottom and had set four men to rowing, and they were rowing, but the boat was so low in the water that rowing it was like rowing a cellar.

There was a young steward who had broken his arm going over and who sat in the corner of the boat with his arm in his coat and his teeth chattering so hard it sounded like a machine.

There was a big man who would not give his name and who kept saying that he was going to swim for one of the other boats, and who said it eleven times, and who on the twelfth time stood up and stepped over the gunwale and went into the water and drowned within four feet of the boat, in complete silence, because he could not swim at all.

Nobody tried to stop him. That was the thing Arwen could not explain to anybody afterwards, and did explain, once, to one person, in a hotel room in New York, at three in the morning: nobody tried to stop him because it had got to the stage where stopping people was more effort than it was worth.

At some point — she thought it was about three o'clock — a man in the boat died.

He had been sitting with his back against the canvas side and his chin on his chest since before they got the oars out, and the fireman went over and shook him and said something in his ear and then stood back and said, "He's gone."

There was a silence.

"Then get him out of the boat," said a woman's voice, from the far end, quite clearly. "He's taking up room and we've not got room to spare."

Nobody moved.

"He's dead," said the woman. "There's living people in this boat and there's water coming in and he's dead. Get him over the side."

"I'll not do it," said the young steward.

"Then I will."

In the end it was the fireman and a man from second class who lifted him, and it was done quickly and not roughly, and they said nothing over him at all, and Arwen, who had been brought up in the chapel at Blaenau with the whole apparatus of the burial service — *I am the resurrection and the life* — found that she could not remember one word of it.

There were three of them before the night was out. She did not know their names either.

A man tried to get into the boat at about half past two.

He came out of the dark without any warning at all — there was no sound of swimming, because the sea was flat and there was nothing to make a sound against — and his hands came over the gunwale between Arwen and the fireman, and he got his chest up, and he said, "Please. Please. I've a wife and—"

The fireman hit him.

He did it with the flat of an oar, without saying anything, without even appearing to decide to, and the man went back into the water and came up a moment later three feet away and did not try again, and nobody in the boat said one word.

Arwen heard him for a while. Not shouting. Breathing, mostly, and once he said, "Oh," in a small surprised voice, and then he was quiet, and the sea took him down without any fuss at all.

"Why did you do that?" she said.

The fireman looked at her. He was a big man with a coal-black face and red-rimmed eyes and no coat, and he said, "Because there's thirty in this boat and there's room for twelve and if one gets in they'll all come and we'll all be in the water. That's why."

"He had a wife."

"Aye," said the fireman. "So did I."

---

Later — much later, in a room in New York with the curtains shut against the morning, talking to a man who had been in the boat with her — she asked him what he had thought about, in the water, in the dark, in that hour.

"Nothing," he said. "That's the thing nobody tells you. I thought about nothing at all. I couldn't have told you my own name."

"But you were awake."

"I was awake," he said. "And I was thinking about a horse."

"A horse?"

"A horse I had when I was a boy in Lancashire. A grey. I've not thought about that horse in thirty years and I thought about it for an hour solid, in the water, on the fifteenth of April. I could tell you the colour of its knees." He turned his hat round in his hands. "The mind's a queer thing, Mrs. Vaughan. It goes where it likes."

---

She thought about the sea at Barmouth.

That was where her mind went, and it went there and stayed there, and afterwards she was not able to decide whether it had been a comfort or a torment or simply the last thing left in the cupboard.

The sea at Barmouth in August was brown at the edge and green further out and warm as a hand, and when you swam in it you could see your own arms going pale in it, and the sand came up between your toes in a cloud, and if you stood up to your chest the whole world was a line of grey houses and a hill and a bridge.

This sea was black and it was thirty-one degrees and it did not move.

There was no swell. That was what she could not get over, afterwards, when she tried to describe it: there was no swell at all. A thousand people had just gone into the North Atlantic and the North Atlantic had not been troubled by it in the slightest.

And somewhere in that flat black water, a hundred yards off, was a thing she could not look at and could not stop looking at: the ship, going.

She had seen it from underneath, for a second, when she went down. That image came back to her for years, and it came back in the daytime, at inconvenient moments, in shops and in railway carriages and once in a church: the *Titanic* seen from below the surface, with all her lights on, going down by the head, at an angle, like a great lit-up street sliding into a hole.

---

There was an Italian woman in the boat who prayed.

She prayed out loud, steadily, without any hysteria, in a low voice, and it went on underneath everything: under the oars and the water and the man who wanted to swim for it and the man who died. Arwen did not understand a word of it. It was not, she thought, a prayer for deliverance; it had the rhythm of a thing said many times before, a rosary or a litany, something a person says in the dark because it is what her hands and her mouth know how to do.

At some point the fireman said, "For God's sake, woman," and the Italian woman went on praying, and a man said, "Leave her alone," and the fireman said nothing more.

The praying stopped after the ship went down. Arwen noticed that at the time and thought about it for years: that the woman had prayed steadily through the waiting and the cold and the dying, and had stopped the moment the *Titanic* went under, as though the thing she had been praying against had already happened.

---

The cold was a country.

That was how she thought of it afterwards, and she thought of it a great deal, because she lived in it for an hour and twenty minutes and it changed the shape of her mind. It was not pain. Pain was in the beginning; pain was the first ten minutes, when her hands went from hurting to not hurting and her feet stopped being hers. What came after pain was a great grey indifference, and the indifference was worse than the pain, because it was comfortable.

She began to be warm. That was the treacherous thing. Somewhere around half past two in the morning, standing chest-deep in the North Atlantic with a dead woman's head on her shoulder, Arwen Vaughan began to feel warm, and it was the loveliest sensation she had ever experienced, and she knew exactly what it meant, and she thought: *I am going to die in a boat, and it will be like falling asleep in front of a fire.*

And then, because she was her father's daughter and had been raised on slate and sermons, she thought: *No, I am not.*

She began to talk. Out loud. To nobody.

She talked about the quarry. She described the whole of it — the pit and the terraces and the little railway that took the rubbish out to the tips, and the water wheel at the mill, and the sound the saws made, a sound like a great animal breathing, and the way the men came out at four o'clock with their hands like stone and their faces like stone and their eyes the only white thing about them. She described the colours of the slate: the purple, the blue, the green, the one they called *lefel wen* that was almost white and that her father had been unable to sell at any price and had loved beyond reason.

She described Nesta as a girl of fifteen arriving at the house in New York with her box on her head.

She described Emlyn Vaughan reading the newspaper in the garden at Porthmadog with his hat over his face, and the way he had said, on the day they married, in the vestry, with his hands shaking, *I shall do my best, Arwen, and I'm sorry it isn't more.*

She described Barmouth in the summer of 1900.

She had never in her life described it to anybody, and now she described it in the dark to a boat full of strangers, in a low voice, in Welsh, with her eyes shut: the shingle and the slip and the smell of tar, the brown sail, the tide running out over the bar at half past two when a boy said it would, a hill in the dark, a slate heart with a grey scratch on it where the nail slipped.

*I'll come back,* he had said. *I'll come back when I've a berth and a wage and something to put in front of him.*

"Harold," she said, out loud. "Harold. You said six o'clock."

The oars went on. The sea went on. Somebody at the far end of the boat was praying in Italian.

And then, from out of the dark, from a long way off, a voice that was not anybody's voice, because she had gone past the point of trusting her own ears, said:

"Is there anybody alive there?"

---

**Boat 14, 3.10 a.m.**

They had been rowing for three quarters of an hour and they had found one man.

He had been clinging to a piece of what looked like a door, and he had been in the water for fifty minutes and he was not making any noise at all, and they had nearly gone past him. One of the men had seen the white of his hand. They got him in over the gunwale and he lay in the bottom of the boat and said, over and over, "Thank you. Thank you. Thank you," until Lowe told him to be quiet and save his breath.

There was nothing else. That was the thing. They had rowed three quarters of a mile into the middle of a thousand people and there was nothing there.

The sea was flat and empty and full of things that were not people: a crate, an oar, an entire staircase, a deck chair with the canvas gone. And in among the things, quietly, face down or face up, the other thing.

Lowe stood in the stern and swept the lantern along the water and said nothing at all.

"Sir," said Scarrott, at the oars, "there's nothing here. They're gone."

"There's one more."

"Sir—"

"There's one more. Pull."

He had heard something. He was not sure he had heard something; he had been hearing things for an hour; but there was a noise out to starboard that was not the noise of the sea, and it was a low, ragged, irregular noise, and as they came closer he made out what it was.

Somebody was singing.

Not well. Not tunefully. It was a woman's voice, three or four hundred yards off, and it was singing a hymn — a hymn he knew, a Welsh hymn, a chapel hymn, the sort of thing his mother had sung over a copper on a Monday — and the voice cracked and stopped and started again, and between the verses the woman was praying in Italian.

"Pull," said Harold Lowe, and then, because he was not a fool and because it was three o'clock in the morning and he had been awake for twenty hours and he had watched a ship go down, he said, "and somebody get that lantern up high so they can see us, and shout. *Shout.*"

"*Boat ahoy!*" bellowed Scarrott. "*Boat ahoy! Is there anybody alive there?*"

And out of the dark, quite close, a woman's voice said, in Welsh: "There is."

---

He did not recognize her.

That was the thing that nearly killed her, afterwards, in the way that small things nearly kill people. He had a lantern in his hand and he swung it over the side of the collapsible and there were eleven people standing in it, and they were the colour of the boat and the colour of the sea, and he looked at them one at a time with a face like a man counting cargo, and he said, "Right. Women first. Who's the women?"

"Here," said somebody.

"Get her up. Gently — *gently*, she'll not stand. Take her under the arms. That's it."

They took Bridget Nolan out first, because she was nearest the side, and she was alive and she made a sound when they lifted her. Then two more women Arwen had not spoken to. Then a man who could not stand.

Arwen had let go of the gunwale somewhere in the last ten minutes and had not noticed.

She was aware, in a far-off way, that there was a great deal of noise and that men were doing things with their hands and that somebody was saying "Steady" and "Mind her head," and she was aware that she was going to sit down, and she sat down in the water inside the collapsible, and it was warm, and that was the end of it, or would have been.

"Sir — there's another one here. Sir. There's a woman here in the bottom."

"Then get her out."

"She's not — sir, I think she's gone."

There was a pause. Then a hand — a hard, cold, enormous hand — took her by the jaw and turned her face up.

She opened her eyes.

Harold Lowe was kneeling on the gunwale of the collapsible with the lantern held up beside his head and his face six inches from hers, and he was looking at her the way a man looks at a chart he cannot read.

"Arwen?"

She wanted to say it. She had it ready; she had been holding it since half past nine the previous evening, through the boat deck and the rockets and the water, and it was the only thing she had left that was entirely hers, and she opened her mouth to say it and what came out was a sound like a door in the wind.

"Arwen. Arwen, look at me. Look at me." His hand was on her face. "That's it. That's it. Keep your eyes open. *Keep them open.*"

She got one word out. It took everything she had.

"Six," she said.

And Harold Lowe, who had been at sea since he was fourteen years old and had never once in his life cried in front of a living soul, put his forehead down against hers for a moment in the bottom of a swamped collapsible boat in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean, and then sat up and shouted, "Get her over the side! Now! *Now*, damn you, she's alive!"

They lifted her over the gunwale and into Boat 14 and laid her in the bottom of it between two women, and somebody put a coat over her and somebody else put a flask to her mouth and she choked on it.

Through all of it she could hear him. He was not three feet away and he was not speaking to her at all; he was standing up in the stern of the boat with the lantern in his hand, doing his sums.

"Sir, there's still three in her."

"Three?"

"Three that's gone, sir. Dead. There's three dead in her and there's one fellow here says he can't move his legs—"

"Then leave the dead," said Harold Lowe. "Take the living. Is there any more living?"

"No, sir."

"Then that's it. Cast off. Give way together."

"Sir — the collapsible—"

"Leave her," he said. "She's a wreck and she's full of the dead and there's a ship coming with a light on her, and if you've any sense in your head you'll look at that light and pull for it."

Arwen turned her head. She did it slowly, and it cost her, and she was to remember it as the first voluntary movement she made after the *Titanic* went under.

Away to the east, low down, out of the black, there was a light.

It was not a star. It was yellow and it was steady and it was coming up over the edge of the world, and as she watched, another one came up beside it, and then another, and then a rocket went up from wherever the light was and burst into white stars and hung there and drifted down.

"That's the *Carpathia*," said Harold Lowe, to nobody in particular, in a voice that had gone entirely flat. "That's the *Carpathia*, and she's seventeen miles off, and we've four hours of rowing in front of us and a boat full of dead men's coats. Give way together. *Give way.*"

The oars went into the water. The boat began to move.

Arwen lay in the bottom of it with her cheek against somebody's wet skirt, and above her the sky was going grey at the edge, and she could see the shape of him against it — a man standing up in a boat with his cap gone and his hair in his eyes, rowing, and shouting at other men to row, and she thought, with the last clear thought she had for several hours:

*There. That's him. That's what he is.*

Then she closed her eyes, and the grey came down, and somebody was rubbing her hands, and somebody else was saying, "Stay awake, ma'am, please stay awake," and she went away for a while into the warm country and did not come back until the sun was up.

---

# IX

## Out of the Dark

### 15–18 April 1912

She woke because somebody was putting a cup against her teeth.

"Drink it. It's beef tea and it's filthy and you'll drink it anyway."

She drank it. It was filthy. It was also the second best thing she had ever been given in her life, after a tin cup of coffee under the after bridge at six o'clock on a Saturday morning that was now two hundred years ago.

The light was grey and enormous. She was lying in the bottom of a boat with a coat over her and another coat under her head and a third coat over her legs, and the boat was full of people who looked like corpses and were not, and the sea around them was not black any more but a pale flat silver, and out of the silver, on every side, stood the ice.

She had never seen an iceberg. She had imagined — if she had imagined at all — something like a snowball. What was out there was a city.

They were everywhere. Some were small and low and white and looked like swans. Some were the size of a church and had faces and buttresses and blue shadows in them, and one away to the north was like a cathedral with a spire, and it was rose-coloured in the dawn, and the sun came up behind it and made a long pink road on the water, and Arwen lay in the bottom of a lifeboat in the middle of it all and thought, with a clarity that frightened her, *it is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen and I hate it and I shall hate it until I die.*

"Morning," said a woman beside her.

It was Bridget Nolan. Her hair had dried into a kind of nest and her lips were cracked and one of her hands was wrapped in somebody's handkerchief, and she was alive.

"Morning," said Arwen.

"Did you mean it? About the bicycle?"

"Every word."

"Right," said Bridget Nolan, and began to cry, and then stopped, and said, "Are we going to be all right?"

"Yes," said Arwen.

"Are you sure?"

"No," said Arwen. "But I've been saying it for four days and I'm not going to stop now."

She turned her head. He was in the stern, still standing, with his hand on the tiller and his eyes on the light to the east — except it was not a light any more, it was a ship, a small black ship with two funnels and a great deal of smoke, and men were rowing toward it in a line, five boats strung out across a mile of silver water like beads.

He had his jacket off and it was round some woman's shoulders. He had blood on his shirt from somebody else's hand. He was not looking at Arwen at all.

---

They came alongside the *Carpathia* at half past eight in the morning, and getting aboard was worse than anything that had gone before.

The ship was rolling. She was a small ship and she had taken on seven hundred people and she was rolling in a way that the *Titanic* had never rolled, and the boats went alongside and came down and came up and bumped, and the women had to be lifted in a bosun's chair or on a rope, and there was a sailor in the chair with each of them, and the whole thing took a quarter of an hour per person, and Arwen, who could not stand, was hoisted up the side of the *Carpathia* in a canvas seat with a man's arm round her waist and the sea going up and down under her like a road.

At the top there were hands. Many hands. She was passed from hand to hand like a parcel, and there were women in dressing gowns and men in pyjamas with overcoats over them, and somebody put a blanket round her and somebody else put a cup of coffee in her hand and said, "You're all right now, dear, you're all right, you're on the *Carpathia*," and Arwen began to shake, and could not stop shaking, and was carried below.

The first thing they did was cut her clothes off her.

She remembered that. A stewardess with a pair of scissors and a face like a schoolmistress, saying, "I'm sorry, madam, I'll have to cut them, they'll not come off any other way," and Arwen said, "They're Worth," and the stewardess said, "Well, they're the *Carpathia*'s now," and cut them off.

The second thing they did was put her in a bed.

The third thing was that a doctor came and looked at her hands and her feet and said something to the stewardess in a low voice, and Arwen said, "What?" and the doctor said, "You'll keep them. You'll lose two nails. You'll have a time of it in the cold for the rest of your life and you'll feel it in your feet before it rains, and you're luckier than nine out of ten and you know it, and now you'll sleep."

"I don't want to sleep."

"Nobody does," said the doctor. "Sleep anyway."

She slept for eleven hours.

---

When she woke it was dark and the ship was moving and there was a woman in the next bed.

"Lavinia."

Mrs. Thorne turned her head. She had a bandage on one hand and no hairpins and her face had aged about six years, and she said, "Well, thank God."

"How—"

"Boat 8. I got in at one o'clock with the dog and a woman from Denver who recited poetry at me for four hours." She tried to laugh. "Where were you?"

"In the water."

"Ah."

"In a collapsible. With thirty people in it and no sides."

"Ah," said Mrs. Thorne again, and was quiet for a moment, and then said, "The dog died."

"Oh, Lavinia."

"Drowned in my arms two hours before dawn, in a lifeboat on a flat sea, and I could not do one thing about it." She looked at the ceiling. "I have been in a great deal of trouble in my life, my dear, and I have never been so ashamed of myself as I was in that boat, because I sat there with a dead dog in my lap and a woman beside me with two children and I thought: *there is nothing in this world worth this.* And then I thought: *there is, though.* And then I thought: *I shall have to find out what it is.*"

Presently Mrs. Thorne said, "Nesta?"

"I put her in a boat at midnight. Boat 12, I think. Or 10. I don't know."

"Then we'll find her."

"She'll be all right," said Arwen, and heard her own voice, and heard how much of a lie it was, and said, "We'll find her," and Mrs. Thorne said, "Yes," and neither of them slept for a while.

They found her at six in the morning, in the second-class dining saloon, sitting on the floor with her back against a bench and her hat — her hat, still, somehow — on her knees, and she was awake and she was not crying and she had been looking for Arwen for eighteen hours.

She did not say anything at all. She got up and came across the saloon and put her arms round Arwen and held on, and she was shaking all over, and Arwen said, "There's a good girl. There's a good girl. I told you I'd come in the next one."

"You didn't."

"No," said Arwen. "I didn't."

---

The *Carpathia* was a small ship carrying twice as many people as she was built for, and it was a kind of miracle that she was not a hell, and the reason she was not a hell was that everybody on her worked.

The Cunard stewards gave up their cabins and slept in the corridors. The passengers gave up their clothes — there was a heap of coats and wraps and boots in the second-class lounge by Tuesday morning that was six feet high, and ladies from Boston and Philadelphia stood behind a table handing out tea and broth for twelve hours at a stretch, and one of them was Mrs. Thorne, with her bandaged hand, who had discovered within herself an unexpected talent for organization and was running the whole of the third-class nursery by Wednesday.

There was a service in the second-class saloon on the Tuesday morning, and it was not a funeral service exactly, because there was nothing to bury, and it was not a thanksgiving exactly, because nobody had the face for it. A minister from among the passengers read the Twenty-Third Psalm and then said, "We are in the midst of a great mystery," and stopped, and could not go on, and sat down.

Nobody minded. He had got further than most.

The lists were the worst of it.

They went up in the smoking room, written in pencil on sheets of *Carpathia* notepaper, and they were added to every hour, and the whole ship was in a permanent state of three hundred people standing in front of a door.

*SURVIVORS.* And then names, and then more names, and then a gap, and then names again.

Arwen stood in front of that door for a very long time on the Tuesday morning, and read the list from top to bottom, and found:

*Thorne, Mrs. Lavinia. Boston.*
*Vaughan, Mrs. Arwen. New York.*
*Penney, A. — Steward.*

She stared at the third one for a long time. It was the only steward on the list. She found a man in uniform and asked him, and he said, "Penney? No, madam. There's no Mr. Penney. That's a stewardess. Miss Penney, of the *Carpathia*. Different line altogether."

So that was that.

*Deveaux, Howard. Philadelphia.* was not on the list, and *Deveaux, Mrs.* was, and on Wednesday morning Mrs. Deveaux was sitting in the corridor outside the dining saloon with her hands in her lap, and Arwen sat down beside her and said nothing at all for a quarter of an hour, and then Mrs. Deveaux said, "He went to get the luggage. He said it would be all right. He said, 'I've crossed eleven times, my dear, and I have been late nine of them.'"

"I know," said Arwen. "I heard him."

"He was so sure."

"I know."

"I should like," said Mrs. Deveaux, "to have been sure about something, once, the way he was."

---

The number came out on the Tuesday.

Arwen heard it from two of the *Carpathia*'s officers standing at the top of the second-class stair with their caps in their hands, not talking to anybody, talking to each other in the way men do when they have been awake for two days.

"— seven hundred and five," one of them said. "That's what they've got. Seven hundred and five, counting the crew."

"And how many were aboard?"

"Two thousand two hundred and eight. By the manifest."

There was a silence.

"Christ," said the first man, very quietly.

"That's not the half of it. They've been at me all morning about the boats. There was boats for eleven hundred and seventy-eight. Do you understand what I'm telling you? There was room in the boats for eleven hundred and seventy-eight people and they saved seven hundred and five."

"They couldn't fill them."

"They couldn't fill them," the other man agreed, "and half of them went away half empty, and I've had a woman in my cabin since Monday who was in one of them and she says there was a boat went off with twelve people in it. Twelve."

Arwen stood on the stair below them with her hand on the rail and did not move and did not breathe.

*Eleven hundred and seventy-eight,* she thought. *Four hundred and seventy-three.*

She did the sum in her head, twice, on the stair, and got it both times, and it seemed to her, then and afterwards, that four hundred and seventy-three was the largest number she had ever encountered, that it was a number with a shape and a weight, that it would be possible to spend the rest of her life doing something about four hundred and seventy-three and never get to the end of it.

---

The nights on the *Carpathia* were a country of their own.

Nobody slept. That was the thing the newspapers never got right; the newspapers had it that the survivors were cared for and comforted and slept under blankets while the good ship *Carpathia* steamed for New York. In fact about a third of the seven hundred could not sleep at all, and they came out onto the aft deck at one and two and three in the morning because the cabins were small and hot and full of breathing, and they stood at the rail in borrowed coats and looked at the wake.

Nobody spoke. It was the strangest society Arwen had ever been in — three hundred people standing in the dark at three in the morning in complete silence, and every one of them had been in a boat, and every one of them knew that the others had been in a boat, and not one of them said a word about it.

Once, at about two o'clock on the Wednesday morning, a man began to cry, quite loudly, and a woman beside him put her hand on his back and said, "There, now. There, now," and he said, "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I don't know why I'm—" and she said, "Neither do I. Nor does anybody. Sit down. I'll get you a cup of something."

He sat down. She got him a cup of something. Nobody looked.

That was the whole of the etiquette of that deck, and Arwen thought about it for the rest of her life: that in three days she saw seven hundred people in the worst week of their lives and she never once heard a survivor ask another survivor what it had been like.

---

The *Carpathia*'s own passengers were, without exception, magnificent, and this fact was never properly reported anywhere.

There was a girl of about sixteen from Brooklyn who had given up her cabin, and who was found on the second morning asleep in a chair in the lounge with her mouth open and a copy of *The Prisoner of Zenda* on the floor beside her. There was a man in the smoking room who had given away his entire wardrobe and was going about in a steward's jacket three sizes too big, and who, when a reporter got aboard at quarantine and asked him whether he was a survivor, said, "I'm a passenger, and I'd be obliged if you'd get off this ship."

And there was a woman — Arwen never learned her name either; there were so many people that week whose names she did not learn — who came up to her on the second morning in the corridor outside the dining saloon, and looked at her bandaged hands, and said, "Are you one of them?"

"Yes."

"Then take this," the woman said, and took off her coat and held it out. "It's a good coat. It's a very good coat. I've had it four years and I don't want it any more."

"I can't—"

"You can," said the woman, "and don't argue with me, because I've been up all night with a baby that isn't mine and I've not got the patience." And she put the coat over Arwen's shoulders and went away down the corridor.

It was a good coat. Arwen wore it for eleven years.

---

She did not see Harold Lowe for two days.

That was not his fault and she knew it, eventually. He was one of the eight officers of the *Titanic* who had lived, and there were four of them aboard the *Carpathia* — Lightoller, Boxhall, Pitman, and Lowe — and they were wanted for everything. They counted the boats. They counted the living. They made lists for the captain. They were questioned by Mr. Ismay, who had a cabin to himself and a doctor, and by the purser, and by a man from the American consulate who came aboard at quarantine.

And on the Tuesday afternoon, two of them went down to the forward well deck where the *Titanic*'s boats had been hoisted and stowed, and they took off the numbers, and painted them out, and then they painted new numbers on them, because the *Carpathia*'s own boats had to be identified somehow and the *Titanic*'s boats had to be got rid of, and the whole business took four hours and had to be done twice because the first coat of paint would not take on the wet.

Harold Lowe did that. He did it himself, with a brush, in the cold, on no sleep, and when a reporter who had got aboard at quarantine asked him for a statement he said, "Get out of my road," and the reporter wrote it down and it was in the *New York American* two days later under the heading **TITANIC OFFICER CURTLY REFUSES**.

He came to her on the Wednesday evening.

She was on the aft deck in a borrowed coat with a blanket round her shoulders, watching the wake, and he came up and stood beside her and did not say anything for a long moment, and she looked at him and saw that he had been to the barber and had a shave and still looked like a man who had been dug up.

"You're up," he said.

"I'm up."

"Your hands?"

"They'll mend." She held one out. Two fingers were bandaged and the nails were black. He took it and turned it over and looked at it and gave it back.

"Right," he said, and then did not say anything for a long while.

"Harold."

"I'm not — " He stopped. He put both hands on the rail. "I've been trying to think how to say this for two days and I'm no good at it, so I'll say it the way it comes. They'll have me in Washington. There's a committee. Senator Smith. They want the officers, and I'm an officer, and I'll go and I'll answer what I'm asked and I'll not tell them a lie and I'll probably tell them a good deal more than they want."

"When?"

"Saturday, maybe. When we're alongside. They'll not let me off this ship till the ship's in New York."

"Harold, I don't care about Washington."

"I know you don't." He turned and looked at her properly for the first time. "There's a thing I've got to say and I'd sooner say it now, in the dark, on this boat, than in a week in some hotel in New York with a doorman listening."

"Say it."

"I've been in a boat for six hours with fifty women in it and I've counted them forty times, and every time I counted them I thought: there's eleven hundred people in the water and I've fifty in the boat, and if I go back I lose the fifty and save maybe six." He said it very fast, flatly, and he was not looking at her. "And then I went back anyway. And I got eleven out of that wreck of a collapsible and four more out of the water and one of them died in the boat, and that's fifteen, and I could have got fifty more if the boats had been filled at the start, and they weren't filled because nobody would believe it and because I was one man on the port side with a gun and a whistle and no damn orders that made any sense."

"Harold—"

"There's men on this ship that'll tell you I did well," he said. "There's men on this ship that'll tell you I went back and nobody else did. And I know what I did and I know what I didn't do, and I'll be living with the difference for the rest of my life. So I'm not going to stand here on this deck and be a hero for you, Arwen. I've nothing to offer you but a man who's going to be answering questions in a committee room for a fortnight and then going back to sea in a ship that isn't this one, because I'll not stop."

She let him get to the end of it. The wake went out behind them and the deck moved under her in the *Carpathia*'s short, fussy roll.

"Are you finished?" she said.

"I'm finished."

"Good." She put her hand on his arm. "Harold Lowe. I stood in the water for an hour and twenty minutes last Monday night with a dead woman's head on my shoulder, and the only thing that kept me on this earth was the fact that I had not said a sentence I had prepared, and when I finally said it, the sentence was the word *six*, and you said, 'Right,' and put your head against mine." She stopped, and made herself go on. "I have been given a second go at this, and I am not going to spend it being managed by a man's conscience."

He stared at her.

"I'm not asking you to be a hero," she said. "I'm asking you to be a man who comes back."

"Arwen—"

"Every time. Every single time. And I'll do the waiting, because I've done it before and I'm good at it, and this time I'll know where you are and you'll have my address and I'll have yours, and if anybody burns a letter I'll come and burn their house." She was crying now and ignored it. "That's what I'm asking. That's the whole of it. Is it too much?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Somebody behind them was singing, badly, in the second-class saloon; the ship's engine was going; the sea was going by; the *Carpathia* was a day out of New York with seven hundred and five people aboard who should have been two thousand two hundred.

"No," said Harold Lowe. "It's not too much."

And then, because he was a man of thirty with no gift for the graceful thing, he said, "I'll have to see about a ship. There's a berth going on the *Arabic*, I'm told, if I want it, and there's talk of the *Carpathia*'s people being taken on, and there's my old line in Liverpool." He rubbed his jaw. "I'll not be ashore more than a fortnight at a time. That's the truth of it and you should know it now."

"I know it now."

"And I've a mother in Barmouth who'll have opinions."

"Then I shall meet your mother," said Arwen, "and she may have as many opinions as she likes."

---

On the Thursday morning the fog came down and they stood off the Ambrose lightship for four hours, and then it lifted and the *Carpathia* went up the bay, and everybody on the ship came up on deck because everybody on the ship wanted to see the thing they had been promised.

Arwen stood at the rail with Nesta on one side and Mrs. Thorne on the other.

There were boats on the water. There were boats everywhere — tugs and launches and a ferry that had been chartered by a newspaper and had a brass band on it, and the band was playing, and men were shouting through megaphones, and there were men with cameras on the tugboats and men with cameras hanging off the derricks of the *Carpathia*'s own cargo booms, and a man in a launch was throwing oranges up at the rail and shouting, "Cunard! Cunard! Was it you? Were you on it?"

The fog had gone and the harbour was grey and gold and the buildings on the Battery were coming up out of it, and the whole ship was full of a sound that Arwen could not identify for a moment and then did: it was the sound of seven hundred people crying at once.

"I can see the pier," said Nesta. "I can see the pier, ma'am. There's a crowd. There's thousands."

"Can you see a man in a grey hat?"

"I can't see anything but hats."

Cedric was on the pier. He had cabled on Tuesday — a cable that had come aboard at quarantine, forty words, *thank God thank God thank God am at the pier will not leave until I see you do not worry about anything at all* — and he had been standing on that pier, she learned later, for eleven hours.

Arwen held on to the rail with two bandaged hands and looked at the city coming up out of the fog.

Behind her, forward, by the door of the officers' quarters, a man in a borrowed blue serge suit with a brush in his hand was painting out the last of the numbers on the last of the boats, and he had been told to stop and come and be photographed and he had said, "When I've finished."

She did not turn round. She had made a decision about that in the small hours, and she was going to keep it.

*Let him finish,* she thought. *He's got a great deal to finish.*

And the *Carpathia* came alongside Pier 54 at nine o'clock in the evening of the eighteenth of April, 1912, with her flags at half mast and her boats repainted, and the crowd on the pier made a noise like the sea.

# X

## What the Sea Left

### New York · April–October 1912

Cedric Ashby was a tall, spare, well-made man of forty-four with a grey moustache and good shoes, and he had been standing on Pier 54 since ten o'clock in the morning, and it was now nine at night, and he had not eaten and had not sat down.

Arwen saw him from the rail while the ship was still fifty feet off. He had his hat in his hand and his hair was standing up where the hat had been, and he was looking up at the decks with an expression she had never seen on him, because she had never in two years seen Cedric Ashby anything but composed.

He saw her. He raised his hand. Then he put it down again, because he was a man who did not wave.

It took an hour to get off the ship. There were officials and there were lists and there were men with notebooks, and there was a woman in a sealskin coat who seized Arwen by the arm and said, "Are you a survivor? Are you a survivor? I'm from the *Herald*," and Arwen said, "Yes," and the woman said, "What was it like?" and Arwen looked at her for a moment and said, "Cold," and the woman wrote it down.

Then there was the gangway, and then the pier, and then Cedric's arms.

He held her for a long time. He smelled of wool and cigarettes and the particular starch of his collars. He said, into her hair, "I thought you were dead for a day and a half."

"I know."

"The papers said — on Monday they said the *Parisian* had you in tow and everyone was safe, and I went to the office and I told them all, I told the whole office, and then at four o'clock a man came in with the *Evening World* and it said—" He stopped. His arms tightened. "I'm not going to say it. You're here. You're here and your hands are bandaged and you're thin as a rail and you're here."

"Cedric."

"Not now," he said. "Not tonight. There's a car. Your maid's coming in the second one with Mrs. Thorne. There's a doctor at the house and there's a bath and there's a bed, and tomorrow we'll talk about all of it, and the wedding can wait. The wedding can wait as long as you like."

She got into the car and he got in beside her and the car went up through the crowds on West Street, and the whole way he held her hand and did not speak. At the house with the lift in it, a maid she had never seen before took her coat and cried; and there was soup, and she could not eat it; and the doctor came and looked at her hands and said the same things the other doctor had said; and at half past eleven Cedric said goodnight at the door of her room and did not try to come in.

At the door he said, "I'm glad you're alive, Arwen. I want that said, plainly, before anything else gets said. Whatever comes after. I'm glad you're alive."

---

She told him on the Sunday afternoon, in the library, with the rain coming down on Madison Avenue and a fire in the grate and the wedding presents stacked in the corner of the room under a sheet, because the wedding was six weeks away and the presents had begun to arrive.

She told him all of it. She had decided on the Thursday that she would not tell him a version; she would tell him the thing itself. She told him about Blaenau and about Barmouth in the summer of 1900, and about her father, and about the letters in the fire. She told him about the fifth officer, and about six o'clock in the morning under the after bridge, and about what she had been going to say at six o'clock on the Monday morning and had not said.

Cedric listened with his hands folded. He was, she thought, the only man she had ever met who could listen to something like that without interrupting it with his own feelings.

When she had finished he was quiet for a long moment. Then he said:

"I should like to ask one question and I should like you to answer it honestly, and I shall not ask another."

"Ask it."

"Is this a decision or a symptom?"

She looked at him.

"Because I have been in business for twenty-six years," said Cedric Ashby, "and I have seen a great many men make a great many decisions in the week after a shock, and about half of them were not decisions at all, they were weather. And if this is weather, Arwen, then I should like you to say so, and we shall say no more about it, and we shall be married in June and I shall never mention it again, and I shall be quite content. I mean that. I should be content."

"Cedric—"

"But if it is a decision," he said, "then I should like you to say that too, and say it now, because I have spent four days believing I had lost you and I find I have not got the constitution for a second time."

She sat with her bandaged hands in her lap and looked at the fire, and the rain went on against the window, and somewhere in the house a clock struck four.

"It's a decision," she said.

He nodded. Then he got up and stood with his back to her for a while, looking out at the rain.

"May I say the rest of it?" he said. "I shall only say it once."

"Yes."

"I think you are making a mistake." His voice was perfectly level. "Not because of the man — I know nothing about him, and I daresay he is a great deal better than I am; men who do that sort of thing generally are. But because the thing you have been through is the largest thing that will ever happen to you, and you are going to marry the man who was in the middle of it, and in ten years you will not know whether you love him or whether you love being alive. That's not a criticism. It's a thing I've watched men do in business after a panic. They buy the first thing that's offered." He turned round. "And I should have liked to have been your husband. I should have been good at it. I've thought about it a great deal and I should have been very good at it."

"I know," said Arwen. "That's the trouble with you, Cedric. You'd have been very good at it."

"That's a hard thing to say."

"It's the truest thing I've got."

He almost smiled. Then he said, "The house will be here. The business will be here. I shan't be a fool about it and I shan't write you letters, because a man who writes letters after a thing like that is a man who is asking to be pitied, and I'd rather not." He picked up his hat from the chair. "If you find, in a year, that it was weather—"

"It won't be."

"Then God bless you," said Cedric Ashby, "and I hope he deserves it," and he went out and shut the door behind him very quietly, the way he did everything.

---

The city went mad for a fortnight.

There has never been anything like it. The newspapers printed four editions a day. There were panoramas in the windows of the department stores, painted by men who had never seen the ship. There were sermons and there was a benefit concert and there was a song called "The Ship That Never Returned" which was sung in the streets until people began to shout at the singers. A man in a bowler hat stood at the corner of Fifth Avenue and Thirty-Fourth Street for eleven days selling a pamphlet called *THE TRUTH ABOUT THE TITANIC*, and the truth in it was that the ship had been sunk by a submarine, and he sold nine thousand copies.

And everywhere, in every newspaper and every window and on every tongue, there were the accounts of the saved and the lists of the lost, and the lists were wrong, and kept being wrong, and people went to the White Star offices on lower Broadway every morning to look at a board, and the board was corrected with a piece of chalk, and names moved from one list to the other, and a woman would stand in front of it in the morning and see her husband's name under SAVED, and come back in the afternoon and see it under LOST, and go home.

Arwen went down to the offices twice.

The first time she went for Penney, and there was no Penney, and there never would be. The second time she went for a woman whose name she did not know — the woman from the boat deck with two children, the one who had said *Mary*, who had said it like the whole of a sentence — and she found, in the end, that she could not look. She stood in front of the board for a quarter of an hour and read the wrong names, and then she went out into the street and bought a paper and sat on a bench in the Battery and looked at the water.

Mrs. Thorne had a theory about the board. "It isn't a list of people," she said. "It's a list of the things we couldn't do. Every name on it is a boat that wasn't filled."

---

She read his testimony on the twenty-sixth of April, in the *Times*, at the breakfast table, with her coffee going cold.

It was on the fourth page and it was headed **LOWE TELLS OF SHOTS FIRED TO CHECK RUSH ON BOAT**. It was not a flattering piece. The Senator had asked him about the revolver and Harold Lowe had told him exactly what he had done and exactly why, in exactly the words he had used on the deck, and the reporter had written down the words, and the words were *like a dog*.

There had been a great deal of trouble about it. There was a lady from Philadelphia who had been in the boat and who had given an interview saying that the fifth officer had been very rude, and there was a Senator who had asked him whether he did not think he had been a little severe, and Harold Lowe had said that he had not been severe, that he had been in a hurry, and that if he had not been in a hurry there would have been a good many more women in the water.

Then the Senator had asked him something about the number of boats, and whether he had known how many people were aboard, and Harold Lowe had said that he had not, and that it was not an officer's business, and that he had had his own work to do.

And then the reporter had got this:

> "Are you a seaman?" Senator Smith asked.
>
> "I am a seaman," replied Mr. Lowe.

Arwen read that four times. Then she put the paper down and laughed out loud at a breakfast table in a house on Madison Avenue, alone, with her bandaged hands in her lap, and then put her face in her hands.

---

He came to New York on the third of May.

He had written twice from Washington on hotel notepaper, in a large, ugly, determined hand, and both letters were short and both letters had a great deal in them, and the second one ended: *I am sick of committee rooms and I am sick of being asked what I thought and I will tell you what I thought when I see you. Do not go anywhere. H.L.*

He came up the steps of the house on Madison Avenue at four in the afternoon in a brown suit that did not fit him and a hat that did, and stood in the hall with his hat in his hands, and Nesta let him in, and Nesta said, "Good afternoon, sir," and Harold Lowe said, "Good afternoon," and then Nesta, who was nineteen years old and had been in a boat, burst into tears and had to be led away.

"You've a talent," said Arwen, "for making women cry."

"It's the suit," he said. "I bought it in Washington. A man in the hotel recommended the shop. I've been robbed."

They went into the library. He would not sit down. He walked up and down in front of the bookshelves with his hands behind his back like a man on a bridge, and she sat in the chair by the fire and let him.

"Right," he said. "I've been thinking about this the whole way up on the train, and I've made a mess of it in my head about nine times, so I'll do it the way I'd do a sounding. Straight down."

"Straight down."

"I'm a sailor. I'll be a sailor until I'm too old and then I'll be an old sailor with opinions in a chair. I've no money to speak of — there's three hundred pound in the bank, and there'll be a bit more than that when the fund settles, and I've never owned a house and I've never wanted to." He stopped. "And I've a temper and I've a mouth on me and I've been told by two captains that I'll never make master for the mouth, and I've thought about that a good deal these last weeks and I think they may be right."

"Harold—"

"And there's a thing I'd not have said a month ago and I'll say it now." He stopped pacing. "I'm not the man I was on the eleventh of April. I've had a look at myself in a way I'd not had before. And I'll not swear to you that I'm better, because I don't know, but I'm the man that's here, and I'll not be a different one next year, and that's the whole of what I've got."

Arwen got up.

"That's the worst proposal," she said, "that I have ever heard," and put her arms round him.

He stood absolutely still for a moment, and then his arms came round her and his face went down into her shoulder, and he said, muffled, "I haven't asked you anything."

"Then ask."

"Will you have me?"

"Yes."

"Right," said Harold Lowe. "Right." And then, after a moment, in a completely different voice: "I've to be in Liverpool by the twentieth. There's a berth on the *Arabic* and I've taken it."

"I know."

"You'll not mind?"

"I shall mind very much," said Arwen, "and I shall do it anyway, and you'll write to me from every port, and if you don't I shall come and find the ship and stand on the quay and shout at you in Welsh."

---

They had a fortnight.

That was the whole of it: a fortnight in New York in May, and it was, she said afterwards, the strangest honeymoon ever conducted by two people who were not married, because they spent most of it being ill.

She had a cough that would not go, and her feet hurt in the cold, and two of her nails came off and had to be dressed by a doctor, and there were nights when she woke at ten past two in the morning and could not get back to sleep and had to get up and stand at the window. And he — he was not right either. He did not speak about it, ever, and he did not sleep well, and twice she woke in the chair in the library and found him standing at the window in the dark, fully dressed, looking at the street.

"What is it?"

"Nothing."

"Harold."

"Nothing," he said. "Go back to sleep."

Once — only once — he told her something.

They were on a ferry going over to Staten Island, because he had wanted to be on the water, and it was a bright cold day, and the ferry was full of people going about their business, and a woman near them had a little boy who was blowing a tin whistle.

Harold Lowe went absolutely white.

He did not say anything, and he did not move, and after about ten seconds he took Arwen by the elbow and walked her to the other end of the ferry and stood there with his back to everybody, looking at the water, for the whole crossing.

"That was a whistle," he said at last.

"Yes."

"There was a man in the water. Off the starboard bow. He had a whistle and he blew it for a quarter of an hour." He was gripping the rail. "I could hear it from the boat. It came across the water the way sound does at night. And I had fifty people in that boat and I'd have lost them all, and I sat there with my hand on the tiller and I counted the seconds and I waited for it to stop." He swallowed. "And it stopped."

"Harold."

"I'll not be told I did the wrong thing," he said. "I know I did the right thing. I've been told by men who know the sea that I did the right thing, and I've told myself so, and I'll go on telling myself so till I die." He turned and looked at her. "But I heard it stop, Arwen. I was there when it stopped."

She took his hand. The ferry went on across the harbour and the little boy blew his whistle and his mother told him to stop, and the wind came across the water off the Narrows, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

---

He sailed on the *Arabic* on the eighteenth of May, and she went down to the pier with him and stood in the crowd and watched him go up the gangway, and he turned at the top and looked for her and could not find her in the crowd, and then he did, and he raised his hand.

She went to Barmouth in August.

She went for a reason: because his mother was there, and because he had written to her about it in a letter that was four pages long and mostly about a boiler, and at the bottom of the fourth page he had written, *I have told my mother about you. She has views. H.L.*

Mrs. Lowe kept a lodging house on the front with four rooms and a copper, and she was a small, upright, ferocious woman of sixty with her son's jaw and her son's way of looking at a person as if they were a rope that might or might not hold. She looked at Arwen Vaughan on the step for a long moment.

"You're the quarryman's daughter."

"I am."

"Your father's dead."

"He is."

"Good," said Mrs. Lowe. "Then I can say what I like about him." And she stood aside and let her in, and that was that.

They got on. It took three days and a great deal of chapel and a great deal of tea, and on the fourth day Mrs. Lowe said, over the ironing, "He's not an easy man."

"No."

"He'll not be told anything. He'll go his own way and he'll go it fast and he'll say a thing to your face that another man would say behind your back, and you'll want to hit him with the poker."

"I know."

"His father was the same," said Mrs. Lowe, "and I had thirty-one years of it and I'd have another thirty-one tomorrow," and put the iron down, and that was the closest she ever came to giving a blessing.

Arwen walked down to the shingle on the last evening.

The tide was out and the sand was wet and shining and there were children on it, and the bridge went out across the estuary on its forest of trestles, and the light was going the colour of weak tea, and a boat came down the river with a brown sail.

She stood on the shingle for a long time with her boots in the wet sand.

She was twenty-nine years old and she had been married and widowed and engaged and nearly drowned, and she had a piece of slate in her pocket that she had made when she was seventeen and that a man had worn against his chest for twelve years and that was now so smooth you could not see the scratch any more, and she stood on the shingle at Barmouth where she had stood at seventeen and looked at the water.

"Hiraeth," she said, out loud, to nobody.

It was the word for it, and it was not the word for it. It was the word for a longing for a home you could not go back to, and she had spent twelve years in it without knowing its name, and she had found out on the fifteenth of April, at ten past two in the morning, in six feet of water, that the thing she had been longing for was not a place.

---

They were married in the spring of 1913, in the Calvinistic Methodist chapel at Barmouth, and it rained, and Mrs. Lowe cried and denied it, and Nesta held the flowers and dropped them twice, and Mrs. Thorne came from Boston and stood in the second pew in a hat that caused a great deal of comment, and Bridget Nolan came from Chicago with her Michael and a photograph of the bicycle.

There were eleven people at the wedding and nine of them had been in a boat.

He went back to sea in the July, and she met his ship whenever it came in, and there was a house at last, in Wallasey, near the water, with a garden that grew mostly stones.

The war came and he went, and he was in destroyers, and he came back in 1919 with a great deal less temper and a great deal more silence, and there was a boy in 1917 whom they called Emlyn, and a girl in 1921 whom they called Margaret after two mothers.

He never once, in all the years, said the word *Titanic* in his own house. She said it for both of them, when it had to be said.

And there were nights — not many, but there were nights — when he woke at ten past two in the morning and got up and went and stood at the window, and she would lie in the dark and listen to him breathing, and after a while she would say, from the bed, in a voice that was not quite awake:

"Six o'clock."

And he would say, from the window, in the dark, in Welsh, "I'm here."

And then he would come back to bed.

### A note on the record

Harold Godfrey Lowe (1882–1944) was born at Eglwys Fach in Merionethshire and went to sea at fourteen. He was twenty-nine when he served as Fifth Officer of the *Titanic*. He was in charge of Lifeboat No. 14, and after the ship went down he took his boat back into the wreckage — the only officer to do so — and picked survivors out of the water and off the swamped Collapsible A. He fired three warning shots into the air to hold back a rush on his boat, and he was criticised for his language in doing it; asked at the American inquiry whether he was a seaman, he replied that he was. He went back to sea, served in the Royal Naval Reserve through the war, and died at Deganwy in 1944.

The ship was 882 feet long and 46,000 tons gross, and she carried sixteen lifeboats and four collapsibles, with a total capacity of 1,178 persons, for 2,208 people. She struck the ice at 11.40 p.m. on 14 April 1912 and sank at 2.20 a.m. on 15 April. Seven hundred and five people were saved by the *Carpathia*.

Everything else in this book — the woman with the pheasant in her hat, the steward who was sure it was a bit of machinery, the tin cup of coffee at six o'clock in the morning — is invented, and is offered with respect.

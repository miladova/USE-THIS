## The story

April 1912. Arwen Prys Vaughan, a twenty-nine-year-old Welsh widow, boards the *Titanic* at Southampton in first class, bound for New York and a June wedding to a kind, careful man she does not love. On her first night at sea she hears a Welsh voice on a dark deck and tells herself she imagined it.

She did not. The Fifth Officer is Harold Lowe — the boy who rowed her father's boat out to the mackerel grounds at Barmouth in the summer of 1900, who gave her a brown-sailed afternoon and a promise and then went to sea and never came back. Twelve years, four unopened letters, and one sunken ship later, they have four days to find out whether a thing can be picked up where it was dropped.

Two of those days are ordinary. The last one is not.

## A note on the record

Harold Godfrey Lowe (1882–1944) was a real man: born at Eglwys Fach in Merionethshire, at sea from fourteen, twenty-nine years old and Fifth Officer of the *Titanic* in April 1912. He commanded Lifeboat No. 14, fired three warning shots into the air to hold back a rush on it, and after the ship went down he took his boat back into the wreckage — the only officer to do so — picking survivors out of the water and off the swamped Collapsible A. He was criticised at the inquiry for his language; asked whether he was a seaman, he said that he was. He went back to sea, served through the war, and died at Deganwy.

The ship was real too: 882 feet, 46,000 tons gross, sixteen lifeboats and four collapsibles with a total capacity of 1,178 persons for 2,208 aboard. She struck the ice at 11.40 p.m. on 14 April 1912 and sank at 2.20 a.m. on 15 April. Seven hundred and five people were saved by the *Carpathia*.

Everything else — Arwen, Nesta, Mrs. Thorne, the Ellerys, the steward who was sure it was a bit of machinery, the tin cup of coffee at six o'clock in the morning — is invented, and is offered with respect.

# VII

## Boat Fourteen

### 15 April, 1.10 a.m. – 2.20 a.m.

**The port side, 1.10 a.m.**

There were too many people at Boat 14 and not enough of them were women.

That was the arithmetic Harold Lowe had been doing for forty minutes and it was making him savage. The boat hung at the rail with its bow slung out over the water, and his men had the falls and the oars in and the plug in and a lantern in the stern, and there were perhaps sixty people pressing at the rail behind it and less than half of them were women, and the boat would take sixty-five and there was not one single soul in that crowd who believed the ship was going down.

"Women and children," he said. "Ladies. This way. Come on, now, come on."

A woman came. Then another. Then a steward brought four more up from the aft deck, shawled women with their hair down and one of them carrying a baby wrapped in a blanket that had somebody's initials on it, and Lowe put them in himself, one at a time, with a hand under the elbow, and said, "That's it. Sit down. Sit *down* in the boat, madam, don't stand up in it, there's a good woman."

Behind them the crowd was thickening.

"Mr. Lowe—" It was one of the able seamen, a man called Scarrott. "Sir, they're coming over the rail from the aft—"

"Then hold them."

"There's too many."

"Then hold them anyway."

The ship was down by the head now. He could feel it through the soles of his boots and he could see it in the waterline: the forward well deck gone, the sea reaching up the stairs in a slow climb like something with a plan. The boats forward were already in the water; someone had got Boat 4 away, or half away; there had been a mess at Boat 2 and a worse mess at Boat 6 with the women refusing to go and a quartermaster who would not pull.

And there were still — he did the sum again, and got the same answer, and it was a filthy answer — there were still more than fifteen hundred people on that ship and there were four boats left.

"Sir." Scarrott again, and this time his voice had gone up. "Sir—"

Lowe turned round.

The crowd had come off the rail and onto the deck in a body, thirty or forty men, and they were not rushing the boat exactly; they were moving toward it the way water moves toward a low place, and the men at the front had their hands out and their eyes on the gunwale, and one of them was already climbing.

Lowe drew his revolver and fired three shots into the air over the side of the ship.

The noise was astonishing. It went flat across the water and came back off nothing, and the crowd stopped dead like a horse at a wall, and a woman screamed, and in the moment afterwards there was no sound on that deck at all but the sea.

"Get back," said Harold Lowe.

Nobody moved.

"Get back from that rail. The next man that puts a leg over it I'll shoot him like a dog, and don't think I won't, because I've had a bellyful tonight and I'll not lose forty women to save one coward." His voice did not shake. That was the thing he was proudest of, afterwards, and also the thing he was least proud of, and he never entirely resolved it. "Women and children. That's the rule. It's not my rule, it's the rule of the sea, and I've seen men die for it and I'll see it kept tonight if I have to keep it myself."

A man at the back said something about his mother.

"Then your mother can get in the boat," said Lowe, "and you can stand there and be a man about it."

Somebody laughed. It was a horrible laugh, high and cracked, but it broke the thing, and the crowd came apart, and the women came forward again, and Lowe put eleven more of them into Boat 14 in about four minutes with the sweat running into his eyes.

"Right," he said. "Lower away. Smartly now, both falls together, don't let her cock up — *together*, mind."

And the boat went down the ship's side, and he was standing in the stern of it with his hand on the tiller, and he looked up once.

She was forty feet above him at the after end of the boat deck. She had one hand on the rail. She raised it.

He put his hand flat on his chest, where the slate heart was, and held it there.

Then the boat slapped the water and he was shouting at the men to cast off the falls and get the oars out, because there was a ship above them that was going to come down, and the only question was how far away they could get before it did.

---

**The boat deck, 1.50 a.m.**

Arwen did not watch Boat 14 row away. She turned her back on it deliberately, the way you turn your back on a fire to go and do something in the next room, and she walked forward along a deck that was now tilted at an angle her body had begun to compensate for without asking her.

There were four boats left and three of them were on the other side.

She went forward.

Halfway along the deck a steward with a lantern stopped her and said, "Madam, they're going from the starboard side, there's a boat going at the forward starboard and Mr. Murdoch's got her, go round — round the front, madam, not through the officers' quarters, they'll not have you through there—"

So she went round the front of the officers' quarters, along the narrow strip of deck where the wind came over the top of the wheelhouse and took her breath, and came out on the starboard side.

The deck was wet with something she did not look at closely. She passed a man sitting on a hatch cover with his head in his hands and no lifebelt, and she stopped and said, "There are belts in the chart room—" and he said, without looking up, "Go away."

The forward end of the boat deck was a different country. The bridge was there, and the wheelhouse, and above the wheelhouse the officers' quarters, and on the roof of the officers' quarters there was still one collapsible boat and forty men trying to get it down.

The other one had gone. It had gone over on the port side half an hour before, and there had been some trouble with it, and it was somewhere out there in the dark now, and there were men on it.

The one on the roof was called A. She learned that afterwards; at the time it was simply a great flat canvas-sided thing on a roof, and the men were trying to slide it down onto a pair of oars lashed together, and the oars were bending, and somebody at the back was shouting to the men at the front to *hold it, hold it, hold it*, and a man was up on the roof with a lantern and his cap gone, and it was Mr. Moody, and he was nineteen years old and he was crying and working at the same time, which Arwen thought afterwards was the bravest thing she saw all night.

"Sir — sir, the oars are going—"

"Then get more oars. Get anything. Get the—" That was Mr. Murdoch, and his voice had gone to pieces, and he was standing at the foot of the roof with his hands up as if he were holding the whole thing by main force. "Get it *down*, for God's sake, get it down—"

A woman came past Arwen, alone, with no coat and no belt, walking aft, and said, in a conversational voice, "Have they gone?"

"The boats? Yes."

"All of them?"

"Not all."

"Well," said the woman, and went on walking aft, and Arwen never saw her again and did not know her name.

The collapsible came down off the roof in one long tearing rush and hit the deck with a boom that she felt in her teeth, and then there was a confusion of oars and shouting, and Mr. Murdoch was saying, "To the rail — get it to the rail — take an oar, take an oar—"

Arwen put her hands on the gunwale of the collapsible and pushed.

It was like pushing a piano. It weighed nothing and everything; it moved in lurches and stopped, and the deck was tilting, and the men around her were gasping and swearing and she had her shoulder against the canvas side and her boots slipping and her hands going numb, and someone was saying, "Once more — *once* more—"

Then she heard a woman's voice behind her, twenty yards aft, saying, "Please. Please. Is this one going?"

Arwen turned round.

There was a boat at the forward starboard davits. It was one of the last two boats on that side of the ship and it was already in the falls and it was at the rail with people going into it, and Mr. Murdoch had turned away from the roof and was there, and Arwen went after him without deciding to.

Mary Ellery was standing at the rail with the baby against her shoulder and the little girl by the hand.

She had done everything she had been told. She had come up onto the boat deck at half past twelve with both children in her arms, just as Arwen had told her on Sunday afternoon, and it had been of no use at all: she had been sent aft and then forward and then aft again, and she had stood in four queues, and every boat she had reached had been going from the other side of the ship, or had gone already, or had never been a boat at all but a crowd of people standing around one.

"Mrs. Ellery."

The woman looked at her without any recognition whatever — she had gone somewhere behind her own eyes and shut the door — and said, "Nobody'll take us. I've been to four of them."

"Women and children," said Murdoch. "This way. Madam — in the boat, if you please."

"There's no room," said a man in the boat. "There's no room, sir, she's full—"

"There's room," said Murdoch. "There's room in her for twenty more and I'll not hear another word about it. Madam. *Now*, if you please."

And Arwen looked at the boat, and the boat had room in it, and she was so tired.

That was the thing she had not expected: that at ten past two in the morning on a sinking ship she would be so tired that the offer of a seat felt like an offer of sleep. Her hands came off the gunwale of the collapsible behind her. She took a step toward the rail.

Then she saw the woman's face.

It did not plead. Arwen thought about that for years, and it was the thing she found hardest to explain and the thing she never got over: that in the last two minutes of the *Titanic*, a woman with two children and no coat looked at a strange woman on a boat deck and did not plead. She simply registered, quite calmly, that there were two of them and one place.

Arwen stopped.

"Take her," she said.

"Madam—"

"Take her. She's got two children and I've got none."

"Get *in* the boat," said Murdoch.

"No," said Arwen. She stepped back, and her voice came out perfectly steady and she was never able to explain it. "Mr. Murdoch, I'm going to go and stand over there by the second funnel and I'm going to hold on to the rail, and if you shout at me again I shall scream, and that would be a great pity, because there are ladies present."

Murdoch looked at her for one second. He had a wife and children in England and eleven minutes to live and he did not know either of those things.

Then he said, "God help you," and turned away and said, "Next. Is there anybody else? *Is there anybody else?*"

Mary Ellery got in over the rail with the baby and the little girl, and somebody took the baby from her, and she turned round and looked up at Arwen and said, "You told me. On Sunday. You told me to have them in my arms."

"I know."

"How did you know?"

"I didn't," said Arwen. "Sit down in the boat and hold on to them and don't stand up."

"Your name—"

"Vaughan. Arwen Vaughan. And when your husband meets you at the pier, Mrs. Ellery, you'll tell him that a Welshwoman with a pheasant in her hat gave you a piece of advice on a Sunday afternoon and that she was right."

Then the boat went down the ship's side, and Arwen watched it until it was on the water, and then she went back along the deck and put her hands on the gunwale of the collapsible again and pushed.

They got it to the rail.

They got it over the rail, and it went down into the sea the wrong way up and came back the right way up, and there was a terrible minute when it was half in and half out and men were shouting, and then it was afloat and there was nobody in it and the water was coming over the deck.

---

**The water**

It came in over the forward end of the boat deck at about ten past two, and it came in a way that no one had imagined.

They had all imagined a wave. There was no wave. What happened was that the whole forward part of the ship simply went under, quietly, the way a stone goes under, and the sea came back along the deck in a shallow running flood about a foot deep and *it was warm*.

That was the thing that undid her. It was warm. After five hours of a cold that had gone through her coat and her boots and into the marrow of her, the water came over her ankles like a bath, and she thought — she actually thought, for about a second and a half — *oh. Oh, thank God.*

Then her legs went out from under her.

The deck was tilting the other way now. The ship was going down by the head and coming up at the stern, and the boat deck had become a slope of perhaps twenty degrees, and the water running along it was fast, and Arwen went down on her hands and knees in it and the cold took her breath with a physical blow.

She got hold of the rail. She got both hands on it, and she pulled herself up, and she stood in running water to her knees with the rail under her armpits, and the water went past her and over her and around her, and she held on.

All around her, people were letting go.

She could not see them. The lights were still on — that was the horror of it, the lights were still on and yellow and steady, and the ship was going down with all her lights burning, and the sea was black, and between the yellow and the black there was nothing to see. But she could hear them. She could hear the whole ship: a sound like a great crowd in a great hall, and the sound was made of every individual voice on that ship saying its own thing, and she would hear it for the rest of her life, and in the fifty years afterwards she never once described it to anybody except one person.

Something struck her in the back — a deck chair, a piece of wood, she never knew — and her hands came off the rail.

She went under.

It was not like going under in the sea at Barmouth, where the water was green and full of light and you could see the stones on the bottom. This was black and cold and utterly silent, and she had no idea which way was up, and she was in a coat and two petticoats and boots, and the weight of it was pulling her down and she was thinking, quite clearly, *this is how it happens, then.*

Then her head came out into the air.

She got one breath — one — and it was mostly water, and she was under again, and this time she thought about the swimming bath and the marble step and six inches of water, and she thought about her mother, and she thought, with a fury that was almost comic, *I have not said it yet.*

And then her hand hit something solid.

It was a rope. It was the falls of the collapsible, hanging down from the davit, and her fingers closed on it and slid, and closed again, and she got both hands on it and hung there in the black water with her face six inches above the surface and the water slapping at her mouth.

The ship was going. She could feel it in the rope: a long slow pull, the whole hull turning over on its way down, and the rope was going with it, and she was going to go with the rope.

"Here—" Somebody's hand closed on her collar. "Here. *Here.* Take her — take her hand —"

She was hauled up out of the water by the back of her coat, and somebody got an arm under hers, and she was in a boat — a flat, awash, canvas-sided thing, half full of water, with people standing in it up to their thighs and hanging on to the sides, and the water was coming over the gunwale in a steady pour and there was no floor under her feet at all, only water and the bodies of people.

She got her arm over the gunwale and hung on, and someone put a hand flat on the top of her head and pushed her down — "Keep your head down, ma'am, keep it *down*, she's going—" — and then the noise came.

The *Titanic* went under at twenty minutes past two in the morning, and she did not go quietly.

She went with a roar that Arwen felt in her bones and heard as a kind of enormous tearing, and the sea boiled, and the thing that had been the deck under her feet fell away, and the water all around them lifted and surged and threw the collapsible up and out and away, so that for one moment they were on the crest of a wave made by a hundred thousand tons of ship going down, and then they came down into the trough of it and the water closed over them and came up again.

Then it was quiet.

The lights went out — she did not see when; they were there and then they were not — and the stars were there instead, and the sky was so full of stars that it looked like Frost Fair glitter on a black cloth, and the sea was black and flat and there was no wind and no ship.

And then, from all around them, out of the dark, the screaming began.

---

**Boat 14, 2.25 a.m.**

Harold Lowe had his boat a quarter of a mile off and he had the oars out and he had his lantern up and he was counting.

"How many?"

"Forty-one, sir. Maybe forty-three."

"Forty-three," he said. "In a boat for sixty-five."

He said it flatly, and the women in the boat looked at him and said nothing.

He had spent the last hour doing the only thing he could think of, which was to make a fleet out of a shambles. He had found Boat 12 and Boat 10 and Boat 4 and Collapsible D, and he had shouted himself hoarse getting them to come together, and he had got them tied bow to bow and stern to stern with what rope there was, so that they would be a bigger thing on the water than five small things, and so that if a ship came they would be found. He had put a woman at the tiller of one of them because there was nobody else. He had shouted at a quartermaster in Boat 6 to come alongside and take people off, and the man had refused, and Harold Lowe had called him a name that he was later obliged to repeat in a Senate committee room in Washington in front of a great many newspapermen.

And now five boats were tied together in the dark, with about two hundred and fifty people in them, and the *Titanic* had gone down, and the screaming had started.

It was not a sound he had ever heard. He had heard men drown. He had heard a man scream once in a hold in the Plate when a hatch cover came down on him. This was not that. This was a thousand people in water at twenty-eight degrees, and they were not screaming words, and it went on and on and on.

"Sir," said a woman behind him, very quietly. "Sir."

He did not turn round.

"Sir. Are we going back?"

He stood in the stern with his hand on the tiller and his lantern in the other hand and he did not answer her for a long moment.

He was doing sums. He had been doing sums his whole life — tides, coal, distances, the run of a ship, the weight of a boat — and now he was doing the worst sum he had ever done, which was this: that a boat with forty-three people in it, if it went back into that water with a thousand people in it, would be swamped and everybody in it would die, and that if it waited too long there would be nobody left to save.

"Not yet," he said.

"Sir—"

"Not *yet*." His voice cracked. "You don't understand. If I take this boat into that I'll lose every soul in it. There's a thousand people in the water and they'll go for the boat and they'll swamp it and they'll drown each other getting in, and I'll have forty-three dead instead of forty-three saved. We wait till the edge is off it. We wait till they're — " He stopped.

Till they're quieter, he had been going to say. Till they've stopped.

He looked at the lantern in his hand. Then he passed it to the woman beside him without a word, and put both hands over his face for a count of five, and took them down again, and said:

"Right. Get me the boat's crew. Four men. And any man in this boat that's got arms in his coat and isn't afraid to use them."

"Sir?"

"I'm going back," said Harold Lowe. "I'm going back and I'll take whoever's alive, and I'll do it without a light so they don't see us coming, and if a man tries to climb in over the gunwale and won't be told, I'll hit him with an oar and I'll not lose a minute's sleep over it. Who's for going back?"

There was a silence in the boat.

Then a man said, "I'll go."

And another said, "Aye. All right."

"Right," said Lowe. He picked up an oar. "Unship that rope. We're casting off from the others and we're going back to the wreck, and I want every woman in this boat to sit down and hold on and say nothing, and if there's a man among you that's got a flask, now's the time to be a Christian about it."

Somebody laughed. It was the same cracked laugh he had heard on the boat deck an hour before, and it was the best sound he had heard all night.

Then they cut the rope and turned the boat's head round, and Harold Lowe, fifth officer of the *R.M.S. Titanic*, twenty-nine years old, with a revolver in his pocket and a slate heart on a bootlace round his neck, rowed back into the dark toward the sound of the screaming.

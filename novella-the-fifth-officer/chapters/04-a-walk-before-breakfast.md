# IV

## A Walk Before Breakfast

### At sea · Saturday, 13 April 1912

She dressed herself in the dark, which took three times as long as it should have and produced a result that Nesta would have wept over.

The grey dress had a row of eleven hooks down the back. Arwen got nine of them and left two, and put the coat over the top, and pinned her hat by feel, and looked at herself in the glass and saw a woman of thirty with her hair badly done and her eyes too bright, and thought: *well. There you are.*

Nesta stirred in the outer cabin. "Ma'am?"

"Sleep."

"It's not six."

"It's not six," Arwen agreed, and went out.

The corridor was lit for night. She went up the broad staircase past the clock with the two bronze figures on it, and out through the doors onto the boat deck into a cold that took her breath like a hand.

It was not dark. That was the first surprise. In the north-east, low down, there was a smear of grey-green like a bruise going yellow, and the sea had begun to come up out of the dark in pieces, and the whole ship was a set of black shapes against a lighter black. Somewhere aft, a man was singing — not a song, just two notes, over and over, the way men sing when they are working and cold.

He was where he said he would be. Under the after bridge, port side, in the lee of a tarpaulin, with his cap off and his hair standing up and two tin cups in his hands.

"Morning," he said.

"Good morning."

"Here." He gave her one of the cups. It was coffee, foul, stewed, with sugar in it and something else — condensed milk, she thought — and it was so hot that it hurt her hands through the tin, and it was, without any question, the best thing she had ever been given.

"Where did you get this?"

"The galley. I've a friend in the galley." He drank from his own cup. "He thinks I've a woman in a cabin. He's not wrong about the woman, so I let him think the rest."

"Harold."

"It's a joke. Sit down. There's a coil of rope and it's dry."

She sat on the coil of rope. He sat on the deck with his back against the tarpaulin and his long legs out in front of him, and for a while they drank their coffee and said nothing, and the sky went from bruise to pearl, and the ship's bow, away forward, made its long hiss into the dark.

"I wrote to you," he said.

"I know. I found out — I found out years afterwards. A cousin. She said there were letters, four of them, and that my father—"

"He burned them."

"He burned them," she said. "I never saw one. I thought—" She stopped and made herself say it. "I thought you'd forgotten me in a fortnight. I thought you'd got to the Plate and seen the world and thought, well, that was a bit of a summer, and I was a girl on a wall with a book."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "The first one I wrote off Cardiff in a gale with the ink going all over. I wrote it four times. I've never been so careful with a thing in my life and I've never been so careful since." He turned the cup round in his hands. "I said, I've a berth on the *Marquis* and it's two pound ten and my board, and I'll be at Barry in the spring, and will she meet me at the station. That was the first one."

"Harold."

"The second one was worse. I'd had a bad passage and I'd got into a row with the mate and I was in a foul mood and I wrote like a fool, and I've thought about that letter for twelve years, wondering what she'd have made of it." He laughed, shortly. "Nothing good, I should think."

"Harold."

"And then I stopped, because a man can only write into a hole in the ground so many times."

"I never got them." She put her cup down on the deck. Her hands were shaking and she did not want him to see, so she put them in her lap, and then she did not care whether he saw, so she took them out again. "Twelve years. I've been angry with you for twelve years."

"I know."

"You don't know. I've been *angry* with you. In New York, in the house with the lift in it, with a good man sitting across the table being kind to me, I have sat there and thought about you and been angry in a way that—" She stopped.

"That what?"

"That I was ashamed of," she said. "That's the truth of it. I was ashamed of how much I minded. I thought, this is ridiculous, this is a girl's nonsense, I am a married woman, and I would put it away and it would come back. It always came back."

He nodded slowly, as if she were telling him about the weather in a port he had not visited. "Aye," he said. "It's like that."

"Is it?"

"It's like having a stone in your boot that you can't get at." He looked up at the paling sky. "I've been in thirteen ships. I've been to the Plate and the Black Sea and the West Indies and I've been ashore in places where the women wear nothing but a bit of cloth and I've been ashore in places where they wear more than you do. And every blessed one of them, the first week, I'd think: she'd like this. She'd have something to say about this." He shrugged. "It's a stupid thing. It's not even a sad thing. It's just a stone in your boot."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Tell me about the ships. Not the ports. The ships."

"What d'you want to know?"

"Everything. I've never once known what it is you actually do."

He thought about it. He was not a man who had ever been asked to explain his own life, and he did it the way he did everything, which was by starting in the middle.

"The *Marquis* was a barque," he said. "Three masts, square rig, eighteen hundred tons. I was eighteen and I was in the starboard watch and I'd a mate called Crouch who was forty and had a wife in Cardiff and a mouth like a sewer. We went out in a gale in October and we were eleven days getting round Ushant and I was sick for four of them and Crouch stood over me and laughed and then gave me half his cocoa." He turned the tin cup round. "There was a lad called Pritchard in that ship. Seventeen. He'd run away from a farm in Cardigan. He could not learn the ropes; it wasn't laziness, he was stupid, he was honest-to-God stupid, he could not learn a knot to save his life. And one night off the Plate we were taking in the topsails in a squall and he went out on the yard and he went off it."

Arwen said nothing.

"We were nine hours finding him," said Harold Lowe. "Nine hours, hove to, with the boats out, and it was dark and there was a sea running and we never found anything at all. And when we gave it up the captain said a prayer and the men went below and Crouch sat down on a hatch cover and cried like a child, and I stood there and thought: *he was stupid, and now he's dead, and if I'd been a better hand at teaching him the knot he might have held on.*" He looked at her. "That's the whole of it. That's the whole of what I do. I keep a ship and I keep the men in her and I try to bring them home, and sometimes I can't, and the ones I can't, I carry."

"How many?"

"Don't ask me that."

"I'm asking."

"Four," he said. "In twelve years. Four that were mine to keep and that I lost. Pritchard, and a man called Osei who went down a hold on the *Marquis* — that was nobody's fault, the ladder was rotten — and a fireman on the *Sardinian* who went over the side in the Mersey at three in the morning and I've never been sure to this day whether he slipped or whether he didn't, and a boy on the *Belgic* in '09 who I pulled out of the water myself and who died in my arms on the deck, and I was twenty-six years old and I sat down on the deck beside him and couldn't get up." He said it evenly, without any drama, the way a man reads out a list of stores. "There'll be more. That's the trade."

"And you'd not do anything else."

"I'd not do anything else." He looked up. "You'll think that's a terrible thing to say, a man sitting in the cold at six in the morning saying he'd not do anything else when it's killed four men out from under him. But it's the truth of me. I've a hand for it. I've been in a boat in a sea that would take your legs off and I've not been afraid, or I've been afraid after, which is the same thing. And when I'm on a bridge in a black night with a light somewhere out to starboard and the ship under me doing what I tell her, I'm — " He stopped, and hunted for the word, and could not find it, and made a gesture with his hand. "I'm at home. That's all. It's a poor sort of home. But it's mine."

The sky was going gold at the edge now, and the sea had come up out of the dark all at once, and it was grey and steady and there was a line of cloud away to the east that would burn off by eight, and the whole enormous ship was coming awake: a door banging somewhere below, a man coughing, the smell of bread from a ventilator, the sound of the stokers' watch coming up through a hatch, boots and voices and one man laughing like a dog barking.

"The sun'll be up in a minute," he said. "And then I'll have to go. And I want to say the rest of it before I do, because I'll lose my nerve, and I'm not a man who loses his nerve."

"Say it, then."

"You're another man's."

"I'm not another man's. I'm my own, more or less, and I've said yes to a man in New York and there's a ring on my finger and no date in the church yet—"

"There's a ring on your finger," he said, "and I'll not be a man who comes in the side door. I've seen it done. I've seen it done to a shipmate of mine in Valparaiso and it took four years off him." He held up a hand before she could speak. "I'll not do it. I'll not have you in a hotel in Southampton on a Tuesday, and me going out to the West Indies, and letters that somebody else burns. If it's to be done it'll be done in daylight with my name on it and your father's slate in my pocket, and if it can't be done that way then I'd sooner it wasn't done at all, because I've enough to carry without that."

Arwen said nothing. The sun came up out of the sea.

It came up fast, the way it does, and the whole deck went from grey to amber in about ninety seconds, and the tarpaulin lit up and the brass on the winch lit up and Harold Lowe's face lit up, and she saw that he had been crying, which he had managed entirely without her noticing, which was, she thought, so exactly like him that she could have laughed.

"Right," he said, in a completely different voice. "That's the sun. I've to go."

"Harold."

"Two things." He stood up and brushed his trousers. "The first is that I'll not seek you out. I'll not be dodging round after a first-class lady on my own ship, it's not decent and it's not fair to the other officers, and if the chief got wind of it I'd be off this ship at New York and glad to be off it." He looked down at her. "So if you want me, you'll have to say so. Plain. Not a hint, not a look across the room, not a bit of Welsh so the Americans can't follow. Plain."

"All right."

"The second is that if you don't say it — if you go to New York and marry your Mr. Ashby and have a good life — I'll not think ill of you. I want that understood." He said it very clearly, as if he were reading it off a card. "You've been a widow four years and you've a right to a quiet house and a man who never raises his voice. God knows you've had enough of the other kind, if I know anything about you at all, and I do. So I'll not be a millstone. I'll think you did the sensible thing."

"And what will you do?"

He looked out at the sea, and the light was on it now, and the ship was going through it at twenty-two knots and making a sound like a long breath.

"Me?" he said. "I'll go to sea."

---

By noon the card by the purser's office said **519 miles**, and there was a small crowd around it again, and a man in the smoking room was explaining to anyone who would listen that she would be in New York on Wednesday morning at this rate, and that the Blue Riband was a matter of arithmetic.

Arwen sat in the lounge and tried to read *The Wings of the Dove* and read the same paragraph six times.

"You're doing it again," said Mrs. Thorne.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at nothing with your mouth moving."

"I'm not moving my mouth."

"You are. You look like a woman saying the multiplication table." Mrs. Thorne lowered her novel. "Well? Six o'clock?"

"Six o'clock."

"And?"

"And *nothing*, Lavinia."

"That is not a word that means anything."

Arwen closed her book. "He says he won't come between me and Cedric. He says if I want him I have to say so plainly, and if I don't, he'll think I did the sensible thing, and he'll go to sea." She heard her own voice go flat and hard. "He was extremely reasonable. He was so reasonable I could have hit him with the ashtray."

"Ah," said Mrs. Thorne. "Pride."

"It's not pride."

"It's always pride. It's the only thing men have that they can't be reasoned out of." She picked up her novel again. "What are you going to do?"

"I don't know."

"You do."

"I don't."

"My dear." Mrs. Thorne looked at her over the top of the book. "You didn't get up at half past five in the morning to tell a man you'd made up your mind to marry somebody else."

---

The cold came on Saturday afternoon, and it came on fast.

At two o'clock the promenade was full of people in steamer chairs with rugs over their knees, and by four the deck was empty, and the stewards had put up the glass screens, and the wind had gone round into the northwest, and the temperature had dropped in a way that made the ship's officers look at each other and say nothing.

Arwen went up to the boat deck at half past four because Mrs. Thorne had said it was the only place on the ship where a person could be alone with a thought, and found that it was not, because there were three men in front of the officers' quarters doing something to a collapsible boat, and one of them was Harold Lowe, in his greatcoat, with his cap pulled down.

He looked up. He did not smile. He looked at her for exactly as long as a man can look at a woman without it being noticed, and then he looked down at the boat again.

She went and stood by the rail fifty feet away and looked at the sea, and after a while he came over, wiping his hands on a rag, and stood beside her, a good four feet off, and looked at the sea too.

"It's turning cold," he said.

"I noticed."

"Tomorrow'll be worse. There's ice about."

"Ice."

"Not here. Well south of here." He nodded at the water. "We've had reports all day. The *Caronia*, the *Baltic*, a German boat. There's a field of it off the Grand Banks, and the current brings the bergs down into the lane, and there's no moon to speak of this week, so a man has to keep his eyes open." He said it the way a man says there might be rain. "The captain's taken her a bit south. We'll be all right."

"Are we going very fast?"

"Twenty-two knots. Near enough." He glanced at her. "Why?"

"Because a woman in the lounge this afternoon said we could go faster and I wondered why we didn't."

"Because at twenty-two knots you can stop her in about half a mile, and at twenty-four you can't stop her in time to do anything but say a prayer." He shrugged. "And because it costs coal, and Mr. Ismay is on board, and Mr. Ismay has a great many opinions about coal." He said this last with the precise flatness of a man who has decided not to say the rest of it.

They stood in silence. The wind took the edge of the rag he was holding and flapped it.

"Harold."

"Don't." He said it quickly, quietly. "Not out here. There's a man in the crow's nest can see the whole of this deck and there's another one on the bridge." He folded the rag. "Tomorrow's Sunday. There's service at half past ten in the saloon and I'm not on watch till six in the evening. If you want to speak to me, speak to me tomorrow, and I'll find a way to be somewhere." He turned. "I've to go."

"Harold—"

But he was already walking, and a man was calling him from the front of the boat deck, and he raised a hand and went, and that was that.

---

Nesta was waiting for her in the cabin with her arms folded, which was a bad sign.

"What?"

"Nothing, ma'am."

"That's your nothing face. You've had your nothing face since Thursday."

"I don't have a nothing face."

"You've got a face like a chapel on a wet Tuesday." Nesta picked up a hairbrush and put it down again. "Mrs. Thorne's maid says you were up on the boat deck before six this morning talking to an officer."

"Mrs. Thorne's maid wants a smack."

"Mrs. Thorne's maid is forty and she's been in service since she was twelve and she's kind, and she was worried about you, and I was worried about you, and there's talk below in the first-class pantry which I won't repeat because it's beneath me." Nesta's chin came up. "You're to be married in June."

"I know when I'm to be married."

"To a man who's never been anything but decent to you, and my cousin Rhys says in New York a woman in your position—"

"Nesta." Arwen sat down on the bed. "Sit down. No — sit down, I'm not going to have a conversation with your apron."

Nesta sat, very upright, on the edge of a chair.

"His name is Harold Lowe," said Arwen, "and he's the fifth officer of this ship, and I knew him when I was seventeen in Barmouth, and he asked me to wait for him and I said I would, and my father burned his letters and I married Mr. Vaughan instead." She said it without any drama at all, because she had discovered that morning that the drama had gone out of it and left something harder in its place. "And that's the whole of it. There's nothing improper. I've been up on a boat deck in the cold at six in the morning with a cup of coffee and a man I've not seen in twelve years, and if that's a scandal then the world has run mad."

Nesta was silent for a long moment.

"Did he keep the heart?" she said.

Arwen stared at her.

"The bit of slate," said Nesta, patiently, as to a child. "You told me about it when I first came to you, the second week, when you'd had the sherry. You said you'd made a heart out of a bit of slate and given it to a boy and he'd gone to sea and you'd never heard from him again. You said it was the stupidest thing you'd ever done and you'd do it again tomorrow." She smoothed her apron. "Did he keep it?"

"Yes."

"Then he's not a fool," said Nesta, "whatever else he is," and got up and went to run her a bath.

---

That night the clocks went back forty-seven minutes, and Arwen lay in the dark and listened to the ship, and thought about plain speaking.

It was such a small thing, in the end. Three words. She had said them to a boy on a hill in the dark when she was seventeen and had not had to think about them at all, and now she was twenty-nine and would be thirty in the spring, and she lay in a bed the size of a pony trap on the largest ship in the world and rehearsed a sentence the way she had once rehearsed the collects for the chapel.

*I want you. I've always wanted you. I'll go to New York and I'll tell Cedric the truth and I'll come back.*

Or: *I'll not marry him. I don't know what happens next. I'll not marry him.*

Or, simplest, worst, most true: *Harold. I've been angry with you for twelve years and I'd rather be angry with you than be happy with anyone else.*

She turned over. The ship rolled a hand's breadth and came back.

At the bottom of the trunk, wrapped in a chemise she never wore, there was a sheet of notepaper with the White Star crest on it, and a pen, and a bottle of ink that she had bought in Southampton and had not yet opened. She had intended to write to Cedric on the first night and had not. She had intended to write on the second night and had not.

On Sunday, she decided, she would write to him. And then she would go up to the boat deck at six o'clock, and she would find Harold Lowe, and she would say it plain.

She fell asleep at last a little after one, with the cold coming down outside the hull like a hand, and she dreamed — she was to remember this afterwards, and to be embarrassed by it, and to think about it for the rest of her life — she dreamed of the quarry at Blaenau, and of the sound the saws made, and of a boy rowing a brown-sailed boat down the estuary with the tide.

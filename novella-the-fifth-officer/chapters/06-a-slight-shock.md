# VI

## A Slight Shock

### 14 April, 11.40 p.m. – 15 April, 1.45 a.m.

For a little while, nothing happened.

That was what nobody ever believed afterwards, and Arwen stopped trying to explain it. There was no alarm. There was no shouting. The engines, which had been a presence under everything for four days, a pulse you could feel in your teeth, wound down and stopped, and the ship went on through the water at eleven knots and then nine and then five, and the water along the hull made a sound that she had never heard before because the engines had always covered it — a soft, steady, hissing wash, like rain on a roof.

She got up. She put on her dressing gown and her slippers and opened the door of her cabin and looked into the corridor.

There was a man at the far end in a nightshirt and a smoking jacket, holding a candle, which was absurd, because the electric lights were on.

"Did you feel it?" he said.

"I felt something."

"Machinery," he said. "Must be. Bound to be, on a ship this size. Bound to be a bit of machinery now and then."

"Yes," said Arwen.

"Do you think we've stopped?"

"I think we've stopped."

"Well," said the man, and looked at his candle, and blew it out, and went back into his cabin and shut the door.

She waited. She stood in her own doorway with her hand on the frame and waited, and nothing happened, and the hissing went on, and somewhere far above there was a noise of men running — not many, not a crowd, just three or four pairs of boots going somewhere in a hurry.

Nesta came out of the outer cabin with her hair in a plait and her eyes wide. "Ma'am?"

"It's all right."

"What's happened?"

"I don't know."

"Should we—"

"Get dressed," said Arwen. "Both of us. Warmly. Properly. And put your boots on, not your shoes."

That was the first sensible thing she did, and she never knew where it came from. It came from her mother, probably, who had been through a colliery disaster in '85 and had never afterwards been able to hear a fire alarm without putting her coat on.

They dressed. Arwen put on the grey with the braid and two petticoats and the heavy coat and the boots with the laces, and she took her jewel case out of the drawer and looked at it, and then put it back, and then took it out again and put it in her coat pocket, and then took it out a third time and left it on the bed, where it was found, four days later, by a steward of the *Mackay-Bennett*, in a cabin on B deck that had flooded to the ceiling.

Nesta was in her coat with her hat in her hand and her face white as paper.

"Where's your other boot?"

"I've only the one on."

"Then find the other boot."

"Ma'am—"

"Nesta. Find the other boot."

Nesta found the other boot. They went up.

---

The corridor on B deck was full of people in various stages of undress, and every single one of them was calm, and the calm was the most frightening thing Arwen had ever seen.

A steward was going along with a tray of something. A woman in a fur coat over a nightgown was laughing at something her husband had said. Two men in evening dress, who had clearly come straight from the smoking room, were standing with their hands in their pockets discussing the matter in the tone of men discussing a delayed train.

"I tell you it's the ice. We've struck ice."

"Well, if we've struck ice, we've struck ice. She's not going to sink, is she?"

"She's not going to sink," said the other man. "She can't sink. She's got sixteen compartments."

"Sixteen?"

"Sixteen. And if any four of them flood she'll still float. It's in the *Shipbuilder*. My brother-in-law's in the trade."

Arwen went up the grand staircase, and at the top of it she met the steward Penney, who was carrying a great armful of lifebelts and was sweating.

"Penney."

"Madam. You want to go up on the boat deck, madam, and put your belt on—"

"What's happened?"

"I don't know, madam, only we've been told to get the belts out and get the passengers up top, and Mr. McElroy says there's no danger but it's best to be on the safe side." He shifted the armful. "Here — take a belt, madam. Take two."

"Penney, is it serious?"

The boy looked at her. He was twenty-two years old and had a Sunday suit at home in Southampton and a mother and a girl he was going to marry in the autumn, and he looked at her for a moment with the sweat running down his temple and said, "I don't know, madam. I've never been in a ship that stopped."

---

The boat deck at twenty past twelve was the busiest place she had ever seen and the least organized, and nobody was running.

That was the thing that took her a while to understand: that the noise was not panic, because there was no panic yet. It was work. Men were working, and the work had the quality of work being done against time by people who have not been told how much time there is.

The canvas covers were off the boats. Men were lifting the oars out and laying them along the deck and doing something with the falls, and the falls were stiff with cold and would not run, and there was a great deal of swearing, and it was not frightened swearing; it was the ordinary swearing of men doing a hard job in the dark.

The steam was the worst of it. Somewhere up forward, the boilers had been vented through the funnels, and the noise was like the end of the world — a roar so loud that a man standing a foot away from you had to shout into your ear and even then you only got half of it. It went on and on. It stopped at about twenty to one, and when it stopped, the silence was worse.

Arwen stood at the top of the stairs with Nesta's hand in hers and looked for Harold Lowe and could not find him.

She saw the captain. He was standing by the wheelhouse with his hands behind his back, talking to a man with a notebook, and even at that distance she could see that his face had gone the colour of clay. She saw a stout man in a dinner jacket — she learned afterwards it was Mr. Ismay — walking up and down by the boats with his hands in his pockets, and twice she saw him speak to an officer and point, and the officer would go and do something.

She saw Mr. Murdoch on the starboard side, and Mr. Lightoller on the port side, and Mr. Moody with a lantern, and a man she did not know at all who turned out to be Mr. Boxhall, and none of them was Harold.

"Stay by me," she said to Nesta, "and hold on to the rail and do not let go of it for anything."

There was a crowd at the rail — a hundred people, two hundred, all in coats and furs and lifebelts, and the lifebelts made them look like a gathering of enormous strange birds. Somebody was laughing. A woman behind Arwen was saying, over and over, in a bright social voice, "Well, I do think they might have told us, I do think they might have told us," and her husband was saying, "They have told us, my dear, they've told us to come up here, and we've come up here, and now we wait."

At the head of the stairs, an officer she did not know put his hand up.

"Women and children to the port side," he said. "Ladies, this way, if you please. Boats are being prepared. Women and children to the port side."

Arwen did not move.

"Ma'am," said the officer, "if you'd go to the port side—"

"I'm waiting for someone."

"There's no waiting, ma'am, we're getting the boats away—"

"Then I'll wait here," she said, "and I'll go when I've found him," and the officer looked at her for a moment and then said something under his breath and went on to the next person.

At about a quarter past twelve, the band came out.

They came up the aft staircase in their white coats with their instruments, eight of them, and they stood on the boat deck between the second and third funnels where there was room, and they played. It was not a hymn. It was a ragtime tune, one Arwen had heard in the lounge the night before, and it was being played rather well and rather quietly and entirely without irony, and the effect on the crowd was extraordinary: three hundred people who had been standing in silence at the rail turned round and began, some of them, to smile.

A woman beside Arwen said, "Oh, good. They've got the band up."

"Yes," said Arwen.

"That's a good sign, isn't it? If they've got the band up." She was a small woman in a sealskin coat with her hair done for dinner and no hat. "It stands to reason. They wouldn't have the band up if it was going to be—"

"No," said Arwen. "They wouldn't."

The woman nodded, satisfied, and went on listening to the music with her head slightly on one side, and Arwen stood beside her and listened to the music too, and neither of them said anything else, and the band played on while the water came up the forward deck and the rockets went up out of the dark behind them.

---

The first rocket went up at about a quarter to one.

It went up from the starboard side with a *thump* and a long hiss and then burst overhead in a shower of white stars, and the whole deck turned its face up to look at it, and a woman said, "Ohh," the way people say it at fireworks, and somebody else applauded, two or three claps, and then stopped.

The second one went up four minutes later.

By the third one, nobody was applauding.

---

She found him at twenty past midnight, or he found her.

She was at the port rail by then, because an officer had been firm with her, and she had gone because Nesta was shivering and because standing in one place with a frozen girl was worse than moving. The boats on the port side were being swung out and the falls were being overhauled, and men were standing in the bottom of the boats with lanterns, and the davits creaked like the joints of something enormous.

And then a voice behind her, hoarse from shouting, said: "Arwen."

She turned.

He had no cap and no greatcoat. He had a whistle on a lanyard round his neck and a revolver in a holster on his belt, and there was a smear of grease down the front of his white jacket, and he looked at her with an expression she had never seen on him and never saw again — not fear; something more like fury, turned inward.

"What are you doing up here?"

"I came up when the ship stopped."

"Why aren't you in a boat?"

"There weren't any boats."

"There are boats now." He took her by the arm — not gently, and she did not mind — and turned her round. "Listen to me. Listen. This is not a drill and it's not a bit of machinery and I've not got time to be polite about it. The ship is going to sink. Do you understand what I'm saying to you? She's going to sink, and it's going to be inside the hour, and there'll be a deal of people in the water, and if you're one of them you'll be dead in ten minutes, because it's twenty-eight degrees. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Then get in a boat."

"Where's Nesta to go?"

"Where's—" He looked at Nesta, who was standing with her hat in her hand and her mouth open. "She goes in a boat too. Both of you. Now."

"Harold."

"Now, Arwen."

"Harold, listen to me." She got her hand free and put it flat on his chest, on the grease, over the place where the slate heart was, and she felt him stop, the way a horse stops. "I was going to say it at six o'clock. I'm saying it now. I want you. I've always wanted you. I'll not marry Cedric and I'll not go back to the house with the lift in it and I'll wait for you however long it takes, and I don't care if it's the West Indies, I'll write to you every week and—"

"Arwen."

"— and I'll not let anybody burn them."

He stood absolutely still. Behind him, a man was shouting for the falls to be let go, and a rocket went up and lit the whole deck white, and the light went across his face and he had his eyes shut.

"Say it again," he said.

"I want you."

"Right," said Harold Lowe. He opened his eyes. "Right. Now get in the boat."

"Harold—"

"Arwen, I've forty people to put in a boat and if I stand here another minute I'll not be fit to do it, and if I do it badly somebody'll die. So I'm going to say this once and then I'm going to go and be an officer." He took her face in both his hands, and his hands were freezing and shaking, and he kissed her on the mouth in front of two hundred people on the boat deck of the *Titanic*, and then he let her go. "In the boat. Both of you. I'll find you after."

"Promise me."

"I promise you," he said. "Now *go*."

---

The first boat on the port side went at about a quarter to one, and it went with twenty-eight people in it, and it was built to hold sixty-five.

Arwen did not see it. She was being moved along the deck by a steward with a lantern, and Nesta was holding the back of her coat, and there was a queue, or the beginning of a queue, a loose straggling line of women in furs and women in shawls and children, and at the head of it Mr. Lightoller was standing on the rail with his hand out, saying, over and over, in the same level voice:

"Women and children. This way. Step lively, please. Women and children."

A woman in front of Arwen said, "I won't go without my husband."

"Where's your husband, madam?"

"He's gone to get the luggage."

Mr. Lightoller said something to her that Arwen could not hear, and the woman began to cry, and then she got into the boat.

The boat was swinging out over the water now, thirty feet above it, and the sea down there was black and flat and the boat hung in the air with the men in it holding the lanterns up, and there was nothing under it. Arwen looked over the rail and thought, with a clarity that surprised her: *that is thirty feet. That is a house. If that rope slips, those people die.*

"Madam." A steward's hand at her elbow. "This way, madam."

"Wait."

"There's no waiting, madam, there's a boat going."

"Then let it go," said Arwen. "There are women behind me."

She stepped back out of the line and put her hand on Nesta's shoulder and pushed her forward.

"Go on."

"Ma'am?"

"Get in the boat."

"Not without you."

"Nesta." Arwen took her by both arms. "You're nineteen years old and you can't swim and I can and I'm not going to stand here arguing with a girl from Blaenau while the ship goes down. Get in the boat."

"Come with me!"

"I'll come in the next one."

"Ma'am—"

"There's a good girl," said Arwen, and kissed her on the forehead, and gave her a push.

Nesta went over the rail into the boat and turned round and held out her hand, and Arwen did not take it, and the boat went down the ship's side with the falls creaking and the women crying and one man's voice saying "Easy — *easy* — hold her —" and Nesta's face getting smaller and smaller, white against the black water, with her mouth open, shouting something that Arwen could not hear over the steam and the shouting and the sea.

Twenty-eight people went in that boat. There were spaces for thirty-seven more.

At the rail beside Arwen, a woman in a black coat with diamonds in her ears said, "They're not even half full. Why aren't they filling them?"

"Because there's nobody to fill them with," said Arwen. "Everybody's standing here saying it can't sink."

---

By one o'clock the ship had begun to go down by the head, and it was not a thing you could argue with any more.

You could see it in the waterline. You could see it along the deck, which had begun to slope, so that the boats forward were lower over the sea than the boats aft, and men were having to be careful how they walked. You could see it in the fact that the well deck forward, where the third-class passengers had been kept back behind gates for an hour and a half, was now under water, and that the people who had been behind those gates were coming up the decks in a river, hundreds of them, with their bundles and their children and their faces.

Arwen stood at the rail by the second boat and watched them come, and something in her chest turned over and would not turn back.

They were so *quiet*. That was the thing she never got over. She had expected — she did not know what she had expected. She had expected panic, and instead there was a great slow quiet river of people coming up out of the dark with their babies on their shoulders, and the ones at the front had begun to understand what was happening, and the ones at the back had not, and the ones in the middle were being told by the ones in front.

A woman came past her carrying a suitcase and a child and a birdcage with no bird in it, and Arwen thought, with a lurch that was almost nausea: *Queenstown. The boy with the cage.*

"Madam." It was Mr. Lightoller, and his face was grey with cold and effort. "You've been standing here an hour. Get in the boat."

"There are women coming up from below."

"There are women here now, and the boat is going, and I'll not stand arguing—"

"Then don't argue," said Arwen. "Put them in. I'll wait for the next one."

Lightoller looked at her for a moment. Something crossed his face — not admiration, she thought; irritation, and something under it.

"Then get out of the way of the rail," he said, "and stay out of it," and turned away.

That was the moment, she decided later, when it became certain. Not the water, not the rockets, not the guns. It was an officer telling her to get out of the way: it was the exact moment at which she stopped being a passenger and became part of the ship.

---

Up forward, a man was shouting himself hoarse about the collapsibles.

Arwen went forward along the sloping deck, holding on to the rail, past the second boat and the third, and the crowd here had thinned and the ship's crew had thickened, and there were men on the roof of the officers' quarters with ropes, hauling at something.

He was there. She saw him before he saw her: standing on top of the officers' quarters with his jacket off and his shirt sleeves rolled, hauling on a rope with three other men, and the collapsible boat was coming up over the edge of the roof inch by inch on a pair of oars lashed together as a slide, and he was shouting at them to *heave*, and they were heaving, and somebody slipped and the whole thing went back a foot, and Harold Lowe said a word in Welsh that made even Arwen blink.

Then they got it over, and it dropped onto the deck with a boom, and he stood up and wiped his forehead with his forearm and looked down the deck and saw her.

He came down off the roof in one movement, swinging off the edge, and landed badly, and did not care.

"What are you still doing here?"

"There's no room in the boats."

"There's room in the boats. There's room in every blessed boat on this ship, there's room for four hundred more people and they're going away half empty because nobody'll believe—" He stopped himself. "Where's the girl?"

"In a boat."

"Right. Good." He looked at her, and his jaw worked. "Go aft. There's boats aft. There's a boat going from the after end, the collapsible, they're getting it off the roof—"

"Harold, the water's coming up the deck."

"Then go up the boat deck and stay there and when a boat goes, get in it, and don't ask anybody's permission." He took her by the shoulders. "Arwen. I'm going to be in a boat. I've been told off to the port side and I'll be in one of them before this is over, and when I am, I'll come back. Do you hear me? I'll come back for the ones in the water. And if you're in the water, I'll find you."

"Harold—"

"I've never broken a promise to you," he said. "I'm not starting tonight."

Then a man was shouting his name from forward, and he let go of her, and ran.

---

At twenty past one the water reached the forward well deck and began to come up the stairs.

At half past one the *Titanic* was down by the head with the sea over her forecastle, and the band had stopped playing in the lounge and had come out onto the boat deck and was playing a ragtime tune that nobody could hear.

At twenty-five to two, Arwen Vaughan, aged twenty-nine years and one month, stood at the after end of the boat deck of the *Titanic*, holding on to a rail that was now tilted at an angle she could feel in her knees, and watched a white boat go down the ship's side into the dark, and in the bottom of it, standing with his hand on the tiller and his cap gone and his hair in his eyes, was Harold Lowe.

He looked up. He was forty feet away and going down, and she saw him look up, and she raised her hand.

He did not wave. He put his hand flat on his chest, over the heart, and held it there — and then the boat reached the water with a slap, and the falls were let go, and the oars came out, and Boat 14 pulled away from the side of the *Titanic* into the black water at twenty-five minutes to two in the morning.

Arwen watched it go. Then she turned round and looked aft along the deck, at the sloping ship, at the people, at the boats that were left.

There was one. A collapsible, up on the roof of the officers' quarters, and men were trying to get it down, and it was too heavy, and the ship was going down by the head, and the water was coming up the deck behind them like a hand.

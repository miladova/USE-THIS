# II

## The Quarry and the Sea

### Merionethshire · Summer 1900

They came to Barmouth on the second Saturday in July, as they had come every year since Arwen was six, and the arrival was always the same: a fly from the station with the trunks roped on behind, her mother holding her hat, Ifan hanging out of the window like a dog, and her father sitting bolt upright with his hands on the head of his stick, reading the town as if it were a balance sheet.

"Brynhyfryd," he said. "Same house."

"Same house, Rhys," said her mother.

"Same house, same smell of kippers, same woman in the kitchen who can't do a chop without drowning it." He said this with tremendous satisfaction. Rhys Prys complained about his holidays the way other men praised them, and everyone in the family understood that this was his way of being happy, and accommodated it.

Arwen sat with her back to the horse and watched the sea come at her in pieces between the houses. She was seventeen. She had been seventeen for four months and was in that condition of life in which every single thing — a shop sign, a gull, the particular green of a wave — arrived with an unbearable amount of meaning attached to it, and she had no idea what to do with any of it, and it made her want to bite people.

"Look," said Ifan. "Look, there's the bridge. Arwen. Arwen, look."

"I'm looking."

"You're not looking, you're looking at nothing."

"I am looking at the bridge."

The Barmouth bridge went out across the mouth of the Mawddach on its forest of timber trestles, a mile of it, black against the water, and the tide was out so that the sand below it was a shine of wet brown as far as the eye could see, and there were people on it, small as fleas, walking out to the middle for no reason at all except that it was there and it was Sunday tomorrow and they had one day left.

The house was on the hill behind the town, above the chapel, with a garden that grew mostly stones. Arwen had the back room that year, which was the worst room in the house, because Ifan had put in a claim based on the fact that he was twelve and needed air. But the worst room in the house had a window that faced the estuary, and at half past nine that first night, when the light had gone the colour of weak tea, she sat in the window seat with her knees up and watched a boat come down the river with the tide.

It came slowly, under a brown sail, and there was a boy in it, standing, doing something with the sheet, and he was singing — she could hear it, faintly, across two hundred yards of evening, and it was Welsh, and it was not a song she knew.

That was the first time. She did not know it was the first time. She thought it was a boat.

---

The next Wednesday her father hired a boat and a boy to row them out to the bar for the mackerel, because it was what one did, and it cost a shilling and the boy was recommended by the landlord of the Fanny Talbot as a lad who knew the water.

The boy was waiting on the shingle with his cap pushed back and his sleeves rolled and his feet bare, and he was not a boy at all in the way that Arwen had been using the word. He was very brown. He had a nose that had been broken and set by somebody who did not care, and a jaw, and hands like a bundle of tools. He looked at the family in the boat — the father in the stern with his panama, the mother with her parasol, Ifan already trailing a line, and Arwen in the middle with her hands folded like a girl in a painting — and he said, "Right. Nobody stand up. If you stand up, you'll go in, and I'll not be diving after you in this."

"Are you the boy?" said her father.

"I'm the boy," said the boy.

"What's your name?"

"Lowe."

Arwen looked up. He was pushing off with an oar, and the muscles in his forearms moved like something under a rug, and he did not look at her at all, and she felt, with a sudden and total and humiliating clarity, that she had been looked at by every man in Merionethshire that week and had not noticed one of them, and that this one had not looked and she had noticed.

They went out on the ebb with the sail up, and the boy — Lowe — sat in the stern with his hand on the tiller and the sheet round his wrist and his eyes going from the sail to the water to the shore and back, always in that order, like a man counting a rosary.

"Tide turned at three," said her father, expansively, to nobody. "We'll have two hours on the bar and then we'll want to be off it. I know this water. My grandfather kept a sloop at Porthmadog and I was on it before I could walk."

"Tide turned at half past two," said Lowe.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Half past two. It'll be slack by four and running fair by half four." He said it without heat, the way a man says the time of day. "We'll want to be off it by four."

Her father's moustache performed a small manoeuvre. "We shall see," he said.

We saw. At four o'clock the water on the bar began to stand up in little pyramids, and the boat, which had been lying quietly with four lines out and a basket of mackerel in the bottom, began to pull at her anchor like a horse at a gate. And Lowe said nothing at all — that was the beautiful part, he said nothing at all, he simply looked at her father with an expression of infinite patience, and her father said, with enormous dignity, "Well. We'd best be off it, then."

They ran back up the estuary with the wind on their quarter and the sail cracking, and Ifan was screaming with delight, and her mother had her eyes shut, and her father had forgotten to be annoyed and was holding his hat and laughing his real laugh, the one from the bottom of him, which Arwen heard perhaps four times a year. And Arwen sat with her hand on the gunwale and the spray coming over, and she was aware of every inch of her own skin.

At the shingle, when they paid him, the boy said, "The young lad can come out again if he likes. There's a crab to be got off the rocks at low water, and I don't mind."

"Can I?" said Ifan. "Mam. Can I?"

"Not alone," said their mother.

"He won't be alone," said Lowe. "He'll be with me."

And that was how it started: not with Arwen at all, but with Ifan, and a crab, and a shilling.

---

She found excuses for eleven days.

There was the shopping, which could be done at eleven or at four and which she began to do at four. There was the walk along the promenade to the baths, which could be taken in either direction and which she began to take in the direction of the slip. There was the mending for the chapel bazaar, which could be done anywhere, and which she began to do on the wall by the harbour with her straw hat shading her eyes and a book face-down on her knee.

He was working. That was the thing she had not expected, and it changed everything. He was up at five, and he rowed people out to the bar, and he carried luggage up from the station for tuppence a trunk, and he took the fishing parties out, and he cleaned the bottoms of the visitors' boats for a shilling and a bottle of beer, and in the evenings he mended nets for a man named Bevan who had one hand. He was seventeen years old and he had the schedule of a man of forty.

"Where did you learn to sail?" she asked him, on the fourth day.

"Here."

"Who taught you?"

"The water."

"That's not an answer, that's a proverb."

He looked at her then, properly, for the first time, and there was something in his face that she would spend years trying to name and would eventually settle on calling *appraisal* — the look of a man checking the set of a sail, deciding whether it would draw. "My father was a master," he said. "Under sail, in the Baltic trade, and then anything he could get. He went down off the Skerries when I was nine. I remember the man coming up the path to tell my mother. He had his hat in his hands and he couldn't say it." He picked at a knot in the net. "So it's not a proverb. It's just where I learned. Off here, and off him, before that."

"And you'll go to sea."

"I've been to sea. I went at fourteen, on a schooner out of Liverpool, and I've been to the Plate and back twice and to Quebec once and I was in Cardiff most of last winter." He said this evenly. "I'm for a berth in September. I've a man in Liverpool who'll have me."

"September," she said, and heard her own voice go small, and hated it.

"Aye."

Four weeks. She had four weeks, and she had not known she was counting until she counted.

---

The summer had a shape to it, though she did not see the shape until afterwards. It was built out of very small things.

There was the afternoon the rain came in off the bay like a wall and they sheltered in the old bathing hut at the north end, and he told her about the Plate — the River Plate, the brown water, the beef boats, the cold off the Horn — and she told him about the quarry, and about the sound the saws made, and about how when she was small she had thought the slate tips were mountains and had been told they were not, and had cried. And he laughed at that, a real laugh, sudden and rough, and she thought: *I have made him laugh. That is a thing I have done that no one can take off me.*

There was the evening of the fair, when the hurdy-gurdy man came and the whole town was out, and Ifan won a coconut and lost it in the same hour, and Arwen and Harold Lowe walked up the hill behind the town in the dark, not touching, with a foot of air between them the whole way, and he said, "You'll be going back to Blaenau."

"At the end of August."

"It's a terrible place."

"It is not."

"It's a hole in the ground with a town on top of it."

"It's the biggest slate quarry in the world," she said, "and my father owns a third of it, and every roof in London is on my father's slate, and I'll thank you not to call it a hole in the ground."

"Aye," he said, "but you don't like it."

She stopped walking. Nobody had ever said that to her. Nobody in her life had said that to her. She had spent seventeen years being the daughter of Rhys Prys of Blaenau and had never once, in any company, said out loud that she did not like it, and here was a boy with no shoes on saying it on a hill in the dark as if it were the most obvious thing in the world.

"How do you know that?"

"Because you talk about it like a woman talking about a dead husband," he said.

And she laughed, and then she stopped laughing, and then she kissed him.

She had not planned to. She had, in fact, planned in considerable detail not to. She was a chapel-raised girl of seventeen and she knew precisely what happened to girls who kissed boys on hills — she had heard it from the pulpit and from her mother and from the ladies of the sewing circle, and the whole apparatus of it had been set up in her head since she was eleven, and it went off like a mouse-trap the moment her mouth touched his and she found that she did not care, that she had never cared, that the whole apparatus was made of paper.

He went very still. Then his hand came up and rested on her jaw, very lightly, as if he were holding something he had been told not to break, and he kissed her back with a care that was somehow worse than passion, and when he let her go he said, in Welsh, "Duw, help me."

"Don't," she said. "Don't say that."

"I'm not much of a one for chapel," he said. "But that's what I've got."

---

Her mother spoke to her about it once, in the kitchen, in September, when it was already over.

She did not speak about the boy. She spoke about the quarry, which was her way.

"Your father's a good man," said Margaret Prys, with her hands in the flour. "He's a good man and he's a hard man and he thinks he's doing right by you, and he is doing right by you, or he would be if you were a different girl."

"Mam."

"I'm not saying a word against him. I've been married to him twenty-six years and I'd not change a day of it, and if you tell him I said that I'll deny it." She turned the dough. "But I'll tell you a thing about your father and you can do what you like with it. He's never in his life been able to tell the difference between a thing that's valuable and a thing that's worth something. He knows slate. He can look at a piece of slate and tell you what it'll fetch in London. And he looked at me in 1875 and he could not tell you what I'd fetch, and he married me anyway, and he's never once regretted it, and he doesn't know why." She put the dough down. "That's all. That's the whole of what I've got. Kindness is not nothing, *cariad*, but it's not the same as the other thing, and a woman can spend forty years being grateful and be a good wife and die with something still in her that nobody ever got at."

"What's the other thing?"

"I don't know what it's called," said her mother. "I never had it. But I've seen it, and it makes people do foolish things, and it's worth about as much as the slate and about as much as nothing at all, and God help you if you've got it, because there's no living with it and no living without it."

She went back to the bread. That was the end of the conversation, and neither of them ever mentioned it again.

---

She made the heart in the last week of August, sitting on the wall above the harbour with a splinter of slate on her knee and a nail.

The slate she had picked up on the beach at Fairbourne, where the ferry crossed; it was green, the good green, the colour of the sea when the sun is behind a cloud, and it split under her thumbnail into thin sheets that rang. She chose a piece the size of a penny and scratched a heart on it with the nail, badly, twice, until the second one satisfied her, and then she bored a hole through the top of it with the same nail and a stone, and it took her an hour and a half and she broke two nails and her thumb was bleeding by the end.

She threaded it on a bootlace. It was not a beautiful object. It was, in fact, a fairly ugly object, slightly lopsided, with a grey scratch down one side of the heart where the nail had slipped.

She gave it to him on the shingle at ten at night on the twenty-ninth of August, with the tide coming in and the lamps going along the promenade and the smell of somebody frying onions behind them.

"What's this?"

"A heart."

"I can see it's a heart." He turned it over. "Is it worth anything?"

"It's worth a great deal," she said. "It's the biggest slate quarry in the world."

He laughed. Then he stopped laughing and looked at it for a long time, and then he took off his jacket in front of her, which he had never done, and he was wearing a vest and his collarbones stood out and there was a thin white scar on his shoulder that she would never get the story of, and he put the bootlace round his neck and tucked the slate inside his vest so that it lay against his skin.

"There," he said. "Now it's worth something."

And then he said, "I'll come back."

"Don't."

"I'll come back for you, Arwen Prys. Whatever your father says, and he'll say a good deal, I should think. I'll come back when I've a berth and a wage and something to put in front of him." He said it without a flicker. "It might be four years. It might be five. You'll wait."

"Yes," she said.

"Say it properly."

"I'll wait."

---

Her father found out on the second of September, from the chapel.

Not from anyone's malice, exactly, or not only. Mrs. Bevan had seen them on the hill. Mrs. Bevan saw everything on the hill; it was her chief pleasure in life and she had been at it for thirty years, and she reported what she saw to the minister, and the minister, who was a good man and a coward, mentioned it to Rhys Prys after the service in the tone of a man mentioning the weather.

Arwen was in the garden when her father came back. She knew at once. She had been seventeen years in that house and she could read the sound of his boots.

"Who is he?"

"Tad—"

"Who is he? The boy with the boat. Bevan's lad, the rower. What's his name?"

"Lowe. Harold Lowe."

"Lowe." He said it the way he might have said *mud*. "And what is he?"

"He's going to sea. He's been at sea since he was fourteen—"

"What *is* he?" Her father's voice was very quiet. That was the worst of it. Rhys Prys did not shout; he had been raised in a house where shouting was what the poor did, and he had spent forty years becoming a man who never raised his voice, and he could put more into a sentence spoken softly than most men could put into a fist.

"He's a sailor," said Arwen.

"He's a sailor," said her father, "and his mother keeps a lodging house on the front at Barmouth with four rooms and a copper, and his father was a Liverpool man who went down in somebody else's ship with nine men and no insurance, and there is a sister who I'm told is in service in Chester. That's the whole of it. That's the estate. Am I wrong?"

"No."

"Then you'll tell me what in the name of God you think you're doing."

She stood there in the garden with her hands at her sides. Behind her, the kitchen window was open and she could hear her mother's knife stop moving on the board, and then start again, twice as fast.

"I'm going to wait for him," Arwen said.

Her father looked at her for a long moment. Then he did the worst thing he could possibly have done, which was not to be angry. He put his hand on her shoulder and said, very kindly, "My poor girl. You'll be waiting a long time, and you'll do it in this house, and you'll do it with the door shut."

---

Harold Lowe sailed from Liverpool on the eighteenth of September, 1900, bound for the Plate, and he came to Barmouth to say goodbye two days before.

They had an hour. Her father stood at the gate the entire time with his watch in his hand, which was a gesture so petty that Arwen could not even find it in her to be angry; she was too busy trying to memorise the boy's face, and the way he stood, and the crack in his voice when he said her name.

"I'll write," he said. "I'll write from the Plate and I'll write from wherever I go after. My hand's terrible, you'll have to guess at half of it."

"I'll answer."

"You'd better." He tried to smile. "Look after that brother of yours. He's a terror. He'll be a sailor himself, you mark me."

"Harold."

"Don't." He put his hand up. "Don't say a long one. I'm no good at long ones."

"Then I'll say a short one," she said, and took his hand and pressed it flat against her own chest, over her heart, so that he could feel it going. "There. That's the short one."

He went out of the gate and down the hill and did not look back, and her father shut the door and bolted it, and that was the last time she saw him for twelve years.

---

He wrote. She knew he wrote; she learned it later, and the learning of it came to her in the way that certain kinds of news come, sideways, years afterwards, from a cousin at a wedding.

Four letters came to Brynhyfryd in the autumn of 1900 and the winter after, addressed in a large, ugly, determined hand to Miss A. Prys, and four letters went into the fire in the study, and nobody ever told her, and she spent two years believing that a boy she had loved had forgotten her in a fortnight.

She learned the shape of waiting instead. She learned to be looked at by the sons of quarry men and slate merchants and to smile and to say nothing, and she learned what her father's kindness was worth, which was exactly as much as her obedience. In the spring of 1902 a widower of thirty-four came to the house — Emlyn Vaughan, of Vaughan & Sons, Porthmadog, shipowners, coal and slate coastwise, a courteous man with a good laugh and grey at the temples and a house in New York that he was enlarging — and her father said, "There. There's a man," and her mother said, "He's kind, *cariad*, and kindness is not nothing," and Arwen looked at him across the tea things and thought: *he is not the sea, but he is a ship, and a ship will do.*

They married in March 1903. He was kind. He was kind for six years, and then he was in Naples on business in the autumn of 1908 and there was typhoid in the harbour quarter, and he came home in a coffin in the hold of his own line's steamer, which was, she thought afterwards, either a joke in very poor taste or the only fitting end for a man who had spent his life arranging other people's cargoes.

There was a daughter in 1906 who did not breathe.

And there were years in New York in a house with a lift in it, and there was the strike at Penrhyn that her father wrote about for two years as though it were a death in the family, and there was a revival in Wales that she read about in the newspapers and did not go home for, and there was a great deal of bridge.

And then, in April 1912, there was a Wednesday, and a ship that smelled like a piano shop, and a man's back going forward along a dark deck at one in the morning.

She lay in her bed on the *Titanic* and looked at the ceiling and said, to the dark, in Welsh, "You never wrote."

And somewhere above her, on the boat deck, a man who had written four times and had his letters burned went on his rounds with a slate heart under his vest and a whistle in his pocket, and did not know she was aboard, and would have said, if anyone had asked him, that he had put all that behind him long ago.
